Delaware Bay meets the Atlantic Ocean through an entrance about 10 miles wide, with Cape May on its northeast side and Cape Henlopen, in Delaware, on its southwest side. NOAA's Coast Pilot calls the bay "an expansion of the lower part of Delaware River," and says the bay and river together divide New Jersey, to the east, from Delaware and Pennsylvania, to the west. Where the river stops and the bay starts was fixed "arbitrarily" by the legislatures of Delaware and New Jersey: a line 42 miles above the capes, drawn from a monument on Liston Point, Delaware, to a matching monument on the south side of Hope Creek's entrance in New Jersey. [@noaa-cp-ch6] [@pde-estuary]

The Delaware River Basin Commission, whose members are Delaware, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, New York and the United States, gives the bay an area of 782 square miles, listed on top of the 13,539 square miles the basin covers in four states. The commission places the river's mouth at Cape May, New Jersey, and Lewes, Delaware, where the bay reaches the ocean. The shape on this map is not the whole bay. It joins the three polygons named "Delaware Bay" in the U.S. Census Bureau's 2025 TIGER/Line water files for New Jersey's Cape May, Cumberland and Salem counties, so it shows only the New Jersey side; a small piece at the mouth, south of latitude 38.9 degrees north, was trimmed off to fit the map. [@drbc-basin] [@tiger-areawater]

The Coast Pilot describes the bay's New Jersey shore as low-lying and short on landmarks. The two main tributaries on that side, the Maurice and Cohansey rivers, can serve as harbors of refuge for small boats traveling between the Cape May Canal and the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal, and fishing boats use many smaller creeks, most of which have bars across their mouths. Water along this shore is generally 7 to 15 feet deep, with many spots under 6 feet and shoals that are mostly unmarked. The bay as a whole is shallow along both its northeastern and southwestern sides; natural depths of 50 feet or more last for 5 miles above the capes, and from there the federal project depth for the channel is 40 feet as far upriver as Newbold Island. [@noaa-cp-ch6]

The basin commission puts the tidal change in the bay at about six feet, against about ten feet at Trenton. The Coast Pilot warns that strong easterly or southeasterly winds sometimes produce high tides that flood lowlands and damage property on the bay and river fronts, and it reports that strong northwesterlies, common from November through March, can bring lower low tides than predicted when they blow for a long time. In ordinary winters, it adds, the bay and river usually hold enough ice to be "of some concern to navigation." [@drbc-basin] [@noaa-cp-ch6]

The bay is part of the Delaware Estuary, which the Partnership for the Delaware Estuary describes as running from Trenton, New Jersey, and Morrisville, Pennsylvania, south to Cape May and Cape Henlopen, taking in all of the bay and the tidal reach of the river. By the basin commission's count, that tidal river runs 133 miles from Trenton down to the bay, and its water changes from salt in the bay to brackish at Wilmington, Delaware, to mostly fresh at Philadelphia and Trenton. [@pde-estuary] [@drbc-basin]

## Why it matters

A U.S. Geological Survey page from 2024 says Delaware Bay supports "the largest spawning population of horseshoe crabs in the world," and the Partnership and the basin commission make the same claim in their own words. The U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service says spawning on the Atlantic coast peaks in the bay in May and June, when thousands of crabs arrive on sandy beaches that are sheltered from rough waves; crabs come ashore on the high tides of new and full moons. The USGS notes that the crabs are harvested for bait and also for their blood, which yields a compound used around the world to check drugs and medical devices for contamination by bacteria. [@usgs-hsc] [@pde-estuary] [@drbc-basin] [@fws-horseshoe-crab]

According to the USGS, horseshoe crab eggs are the fuel many migrating shorebirds depend on. For the rufa red knot, a robin-sized shorebird, the Fish & Wildlife Service calls the bay the principal spring staging area because of how plentiful and available the eggs are there, and it says the bay is the final Atlantic-coast stop for 50 to 80 percent of the knots heading north to the Arctic to breed each spring. In two weeks on the bay, the birds can eat enough to nearly double their weight. The service published its rule listing the rufa red knot as a threatened species in December 2014, and the 2024 USGS page describes the bird as listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act. [@usgs-hsc] [@fws-red-knot]

Sources rank the bay's shorebird gathering in slightly different terms. The USGS calls the bay's migrating shorebird population the second largest in North America, and the Partnership says the estuary hosts the second-largest concentration of migrating shorebirds in the Western Hemisphere. [@usgs-hsc] [@pde-estuary]

The Coast Pilot says the shellfish industry is concentrated on the lower Maurice River, with plants at Bivalve, Port Norris and Shell Pile, and that Egg Island Flats, south of Egg Island Point, are "thick with oyster-bed stakes." Under the year 2005, the Partnership's timeline lists the start of a Delaware Bay Oyster Restoration Project whose first year saw 280,000 bushels of oyster shells planted. The Partnership is a nonprofit founded in 1996 and based in Wilmington, Delaware; it hosts the Delaware Estuary Program, one of 28 National Estuary Programs designated by Congress, and its timeline says the estuary joined the EPA's National Estuary Program in 1988. [@noaa-cp-ch6] [@pde-history] [@pde-program]
