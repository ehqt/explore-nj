The Pinelands, also called the Pine Barrens, cover about 1.1 million acres of southern New Jersey. That is about 22 percent of the state's land, spread across parts of seven counties and all or part of 56 municipalities. [@pc-size] [@pc-history]

In 1978, Congress created the Pinelands National Reserve, which the Pinelands Commission describes as the country's first National Reserve. In 1979 New Jersey established the Pinelands Commission and passed the Pinelands Protection Act. UNESCO first recognized the area in 1983 as part of a multi-site biosphere reserve, and in 1988 designated the Pinelands as a biosphere region of its own. [@pc-reserve] [@pc-history] [@pc-biosphere]

The land is gently rolling and sandy, with low forests of pine and oak, cedar swamps along the streams, and more than 12,000 acres of "pygmy forest," where mature pines and oaks grow less than 11 feet tall. Fire has shaped which plants grow here. Beneath much of the region is the Cohansey Aquifer, a shallow layer of sand and gravel that the Pinelands Commission estimates holds more than 17 trillion gallons of water, which it describes as among the purest in the nation. Its streams are acidic and often the color of dark tea, stained by natural material leaching from the soil. [@pc-geology] [@pc-plants]

People have lived here for more than 10,000 years. From 1765, furnaces smelted the region's bog iron, followed by glassmaking from its sands. The Pinelands' earliest cultivated cranberry bogs appeared between 1830 and 1840, and in 1916 Elizabeth White and Dr. F. V. Coville developed a cultivated blueberry at Whitesbog, which the Commission credits with starting the region's blueberry industry. [@pc-history]

## Why it matters

New Jersey ranks among the nation's leading blueberry and cranberry producers, and virtually all of that crop comes from the Pinelands. The region's protection also grew out of public pressure after John McPhee's 1967 book "The Pine Barrens" became a national bestseller. [@pc-people] [@pc-history]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. About half of the reserve is publicly owned, including state forests and parks such as Wharton, Bass River and Brendan T. Byrne and visitor attractions such as Batsto and Double Trouble. Public land here also includes large federal sites such as Joint Base McGuire-Dix-Lakehurst and the Warren Grove gunnery range. [@pc-ownership]

The Pinelands Commission's recreation guide lists places to go. [@pc-recreation]
