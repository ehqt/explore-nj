Kittatinny Mountain, which the New Jersey Geological Survey calls "a broad even-crested ridge," runs through New Jersey's Valley and Ridge Province, the first of the state's four physiographic provinces the Survey lists going from northwest to southeast. The Survey's circular, prepared in 2003 and reprinted in 2006, puts its elevations at 1,600 to 1,800 feet. On one side lies the Kittatinny Valley, whose floor the circular puts at about 400 to 600 feet; on the other is the upper Delaware River Valley above the Delaware Water Gap, at about 300 to 600 feet. High Point, which the circular puts at 1,803 feet and calls the state's highest point, sits near the mountain's northern end. [@njgs-provinces]

The ridge goes by more than one name. The Geological Survey uses Kittatinny Mountain, while some National Park Service pages call it the Kittatinny Ridge. A 2012 Park Service environmental impact statement used Kittatinny Ridge for something longer still: a ridge of 185 miles that takes in Kittatinny Mountain in New Jersey and Blue Mountain in Pennsylvania and winds across eastern and central Pennsylvania to the Maryland line. [@njgs-provinces] [@nps-birds] [@nps-garvey] [@nps-srl-feis]

Hard rock holds the ridge up. Describing a view southwest from High Point along the mountain's "sinuous crest," a U.S. Geological Survey caption names the Silurian Shawangunk Conglomerate as "the resistant ridge-forming rock." The USGS's state geologic map compilation lists the Shawangunk Formation in Sussex and Warren counties, dates it to the Lower and Middle Silurian, and describes it as mainly sandstone and pebble conglomerate, with minor shale. In its geology summary for Delaware Water Gap National Recreation Area, the Park Service explains that mountain-building over hundreds of millions of years folded and faulted the area's rocks, and that the limbs of major folds support Kittatinny Mountain's high ridges. [@usgs-hp-view] [@usgs-sgmc-shawangunk] [@nps-geodiversity]

The Delaware River cuts through the mountain at the Delaware Water Gap, which the Park Service says has funneled people through "the otherwise-imposing Kittatinny Mountain" for centuries; the gap has its own entry. [@nps-geodiversity]

Parts of the Appalachian Trail follow the crest. A USGS photo caption describes it winding among glacial erratics on the crest of Kittatinny Mountain in Stokes State Forest. The 2012 impact statement counted roughly 27 miles of the trail inside the national recreation area atop the Kittatinny Ridge, and the Park Service's scenic-vistas page points to the Appalachian Trail on the ridge, on the park's New Jersey side, as one of the places with the park's best views. One short way up starts across from the Worthington State Forest picnic area: the Park Service describes the Garvey Springs Trail as a very steep climb to the top of the ridge, where it meets the Appalachian Trail 0.1 mile from Sunfish Pond, a glacial lake. [@usgs-at-stokes] [@nps-srl-feis] [@nps-vistas] [@nps-garvey]

The shape on this map is the band where the U.S. Geological Survey's state geologic map compilation maps the Shawangunk Formation, the rock that holds up the crest, as the bedrock; in many places glacial deposits cover it. It traces the ridge's spine, not the whole mountain with its slopes, and a small separate patch lies just northwest of the main band. [@usgs-sgmc-njss] [@usgs-hp-view] [@usgs-at-stokes]

## Why it matters

The ridge is a route for birds as well as hikers. On its bird page for the Delaware Water Gap park, the Park Service says that in the fall the Kittatinny Ridge "provides an important migratory corridor for raptors." Its scenic-vistas page singles out late summer and early fall, when the foliage turns and big raptors pass south through the valley. [@nps-birds] [@nps-vistas]
