The Wallkill rises at the outlet dam of Lake Mohawk in Sussex County and flows north; the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) traces it through Franklin Pond and through wetlands, across the New York line, and on to the Hudson River. The Fish and Wildlife Service's 2009 plan for the refuge traces that route to the Hudson near Kingston, New York, by way of Rondout Creek. The USGS puts the river's watershed at about 208 square miles of Sussex County. By the USGS's account, some 4.5 miles past Franklin Pond the river leaves bedrock behind and broadens across glacial outwash, and it crosses the refuge before reaching the state line. [@usgs-arsenic] [@fws-ccp]

The refuge's own history page places the river in New Jersey's Ridge and Valley physiographic province, along its border with the Highlands province. To the west stands the Kittatinny range, which the page says continues into New York as the Shawangunks; to the east are the Highlands. [@fws-about]

That page also says the area "is said to have been known" to Native Americans as "Twischsawkin," which it translates as the land where plums abound, and it records at least three rock shelters among the area's prehistoric sites. Later, Dutch settlers moved up the Wallkill from the Hudson. Spring floods left the valley under what the page calls "a huge lake," so the settlers named the river bottomland "The Drowned Lands" and, until it was drained, grazed cattle on its meadows. Work to straighten, dredge and drain the river started as early as 1760, but the page says it only succeeded sixty-six years later, when a large canal lowered the river's water table. Mill owners, the page says, wanted the land kept flooded, and their clashes with the farmers, known as the "Muskrat and Beaver Wars" (millers were the "beavers," farmers the "muskrats"), ended with the farmers winning in 1871. [@fws-about]

The refuge's 2009 Comprehensive Conservation Plan says the Service's director created it by an administrative decision on March 9, 1990, and that on November 16 of that year Congress passed a law confirming a 7,500-acre refuge along a 9-mile stretch of the Wallkill (the plan's summary describes the 7,500 acres as the refuge's original acquisition boundary). A USGS page about a 2004–2007 study gives a different date, 1991, and describes a 4,500-acre refuge. The 2009 plan enlarged the area in which the Service can acquire land to 17,050 acres in total, to be bought from willing sellers as land or as easements. Today the refuge says it manages more than 6,000 acres in Sussex County, New Jersey, and Orange County, New York. The outline on this map is drawn from the Service's national realty data and shows the parcels in which it holds a real estate interest, not the larger acquisition boundary. [@fws-ccp] [@usgs-arsenic] [@fws-about] [@fws-realty]

As the 2009 plan describes it, wet meadows and forested wetlands cover most of the river's wide floodplain in the refuge, while what the plan calls "oak-covered limestone ridges" run beside the river, and farmland and grassland take over on higher ground. Most of the forested wetland is bottomland hardwood forest dominated by red maple. The plan also lists emergent marsh, scrub-shrub wetland and calcareous fens, which form where calcium-rich groundwater seeps to the surface and which the plan calls suitable for the bog turtle, a federally listed threatened species. Its habitat table, counting both acquired and unacquired land inside the boundary of the time, gives 2,098 acres of forested wetland, 1,560 of upland forest, 1,216 of non-forested wetland, 999 of scrub-shrub and 632 of grassland. [@fws-ccp]

The refuge describes its management as having "a special emphasis on forest-dwelling and grassland birds, migrating waterfowl, wintering raptors, and endangered species," and notes that the North American Waterfowl Management Plan identifies the Wallkill River bottomlands as a priority focus area for waterfowl in New Jersey. More than 225 bird species had been recorded on the refuge by the time of the 2009 plan, 122 of them documented as breeding. The Liberty Loop, which the refuge calls the most popular destination among its trails, circles managed wetland impoundments. There, the Service says, thousands of waterfowl arrive during spring and fall migration, great blue herons and indigo buntings are common in summer, the fall sparrow migration is "impressive," and winter brings raptors such as the northern harrier, rough-legged hawk and short-eared owl. [@fws-home] [@fws-about] [@fws-ccp] [@fws-wildlife]

A segment of the Appalachian Trail runs through the refuge. According to the 2009 plan, the trail enters at the Liberty Loop Nature Trail and shares about 1.5 miles of that 2.5-mile loop, then follows Oil City Road to where the road crosses the Wallkill River. The same plan called Wallkill River "the only refuge through which the AT runs." A 2025 draft compatibility determination from the Service still says part of the Appalachian Trail coincides with the Liberty Loop, which it places 7 miles north of refuge headquarters. [@fws-ccp] [@fws-cd-2025]

## Why it matters

According to the refuge's history page, the valley was farm country until recently; dairy farming no longer dominates its economy, and being close to larger metropolitan areas is making it more suburban. The page credits the region's many state and federal public lands with helping to preserve its scenery and to provide wildlife habitat. The 2009 plan said its four expansion focus areas together form a key corridor between preserved habitat on the Kittatinny Ridge to the west and the Hudson Highlands to the east. [@fws-about] [@fws-ccp]

The river also brings in what happens upstream. A USGS study that ran from 2004 to 2007 was started because arsenic in four reaches of the Wallkill and one of Papakating Creek, a major tributary, had been found to exceed New Jersey's surface-water standard; the agency listed possible sources from geologic materials and pesticides to septic systems and leftovers of historic ore smelting. A contaminant assessment for the refuge, updated in 2005 and summarized in the 2009 plan, named Papakating Creek and the Wallkill River as the dominant pathways for contaminants reaching the refuge. [@usgs-arsenic] [@fws-ccp]
