The Hackensack Meadowlands are the tidal marshes along the lower Hackensack River in Bergen and Hudson counties, inside the New York-Newark metropolitan area. In 1976 a U.S. Geological Survey report counted about 23 square miles of tidal marsh, "commonly referred to as the Hackensack Meadows," reaching 10 miles up the river from Newark Bay, and called that marsh largely undeveloped. A 2002 U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service report called the Meadowlands "perhaps the largest urban wetland complex in the northeastern United States," and the state agency that plans the area calls them "the largest remaining complex of brackish tidal wetlands in the New York and New Jersey Harbor Estuary." [@usgs-1976] [@fws-trends-2002] [@njsea-mp2020]

According to the New Jersey Sports and Exposition Authority (NJSEA), the agency that now plans the district, the retreating Wisconsin Glacier scooped out the land between the Palisades and the ridge along Schuyler Avenue, leaving a deep freshwater lake known as Glacial Lake Hackensack. The authority says the lake drained after breaking through at Perth Amboy about 10,000 years ago, giving rise to the Hackensack River, and that centuries of rising seas later let Atlantic tides into the river, creating its estuary. When the sea came close to its present height about 1,000 years ago, the authority says, the forested valley flooded and Atlantic white cedar swamps took over; modern marsh grasses have grown there for "only a few hundred years." [@njsea-history]

By the NJSEA's account, attempts to drain parts of the marsh go back to the end of the 17th century. Its history describes the Swartwout brothers diking and draining in Hudson County from 1816, a project abandoned by the 1840s after tides and burrowing muskrats flooded the land, and later the Bergen County Mosquito Control Commission, which drained about 17,000 acres of meadows in the early 20th century, mostly by digging ditches. Turnpikes, a plank road, railroads and rail yards were built across and into the meadows, and so was the New Jersey Turnpike, which runs the length of the district in two corridors, one on each side of the Hackensack River. The Fish & Wildlife Service, working from an 1889 state topographic map and later aerial photos, found that its study area, slightly bigger than the official district, had 20,045 acres of wetland in 1889 and 5,541 in 1995: 28 percent of the earlier extent. Its figures show the fastest loss from 1966 to 1976, at more than 300 acres a year. [@njsea-history] [@fws-trends-2002]

The NJSEA's history calls the landfills "the biggest change to the face of the Meadowlands." For years, it says, waste was dumped in the district with no oversight, filling wetlands, and a 1970 study counted 51 landfills. A 1969 survey by the State Department of Health, as the authority reports it, found more than 5,000 tons of waste arriving each day, six days a week, from 118 New Jersey municipalities. The authority puts the unregulated landfills of that time at nearly 1,900 acres. [@njsea-history] [@njsea-solid-waste]

The Hackensack Meadowlands Reclamation and Development Act created the Hackensack Meadowlands Development Commission, which became the planning and zoning agency for a defined Meadowlands District. The act is chapter 404 of the laws of 1968; the NJSEA's 2020 master plan says it took effect on January 13, 1969, and the plan, the 2015 consolidation law and the NJSEA's website all say the commission was formed in 1969. The commission became the New Jersey Meadowlands Commission in 2001, and a law approved on February 5, 2015, folded it into the NJSEA. The authority describes its inherited mission as a "three-fold mandate": orderly development, facilities for "the sanitary disposal of solid waste," and protecting "the delicate balance of nature." State environmental regulators keep a role: as archived in November 2026, the Department of Environmental Protection's page said it regulates activities in the state's tidal waters and the 100-year floodplain, while the NJSEA handles land use planning and permitting. [@dep-meadowlands] [@njsea-mp2020] [@nj-pl2015-c19] [@njsea-history] [@njsea-who]

The 2015 law describes the district as 30.4 square miles; the NJSEA's own pages say 30.3. It covers parts of 14 municipalities: Carlstadt, East Rutherford, Little Ferry, Lyndhurst, Moonachie, North Arlington, Ridgefield, Rutherford, South Hackensack and Teterboro in Bergen County, and Jersey City, Kearny, North Bergen and Secaucus in Hudson County. The law traces its edge along railroad tracks, Tonnelle Avenue, the Pulaski Skyway, the PATH tracks, local streets, town lines and the middle of the Hackensack River and Overpeck Creek, and it carves out two areas of Secaucus where the agency has no jurisdiction. [@nj-pl2015-c19] [@njsea-who] [@njsea-mp2020]

The shape on this map is not that line. It shows the wetlands that the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's National Wetlands Inventory classes as estuarine and intertidal, leaving out open water and freshwater wetlands, and keeps only those inside the 14 towns (using the Census Bureau's 2025 town boundaries) and north of a line near the Pulaski Skyway, roughly the district's area. So it is neither the legal district line nor every acre of former marsh. Measured by this project, the shape covers about 4,000 acres. The inventory's metadata gives 2008 as the image year for this area, with image dates running to 2011, while the user report for the same mapping project lists New Jersey's aerial imagery as 2013. [@nwi-wetlands] [@census-cousub] [@nj-pl2015-c19] [@nwi-image-year] [@nwi-user-report]

No landfill in the district operates today, according to the NJSEA. Its 2020 master plan credits the agency with closing more than 50 "orphaned" landfills since 1969, and its website says regulated landfill disposal continued under its supervision until 2019 and that all its landfills are now inactive. One of them, the 1A Landfill in Kearny, carries a 3-megawatt solar installation of 12,506 panels on 13 acres; the authority calls it the first solar farm on a landfill owned by the state. [@njsea-mp2020] [@njsea-solid-waste] [@njsea-history]

The 2020 master plan's land-use count shows a mixed landscape. Wetlands covered about 6,213 of the district's 19,396 acres, or 32 percent, transportation 20.5 percent and industry 16.2 percent. The plan lists Teterboro Airport, about 827 acres in Teterboro and Moonachie; the New Jersey Turnpike and Routes 495, 3, 17, 46, 120, 280 and 1 & 9; and nine rail lines for commuters, long-distance travelers and freight. On preserved wetlands, the plan says about 3,500 acres had been protected as of 2019; the NJSEA's website gives both "more than 3,400 acres" and 2,500 acres on the same page. [@njsea-mp2020] [@njsea-who]

The master plan says the NJSEA owns and controls the Meadowlands Sports Complex in East Rutherford. The 2015 law gives it 750 acres, while the master plan's land-use table counts 580. The authority lists MetLife Stadium, home of the New York Giants and New York Jets, the Meadowlands Racetrack and the American Dream Meadowlands shopping and entertainment center there. [@nj-pl2015-c19] [@njsea-mp2020] [@njsea-who]

## Why it matters

The marshes are bird habitat. The NJSEA reports more than 280 bird species on one page and more than 285 on another, 34 of them on New Jersey's endangered, threatened or special-concern lists. The 2002 Fish & Wildlife Service report described the Meadowlands as "a major link along the Atlantic Flyway" for migrating birds, especially shorebirds. [@njsea-history] [@njsea-who] [@fws-trends-2002]

In 2018 the EPA reported high levels of mercury, methyl mercury, PCBs and chromium in the water and sediment of Berry's Creek, which runs to the Hackensack River through Carlstadt, East Rutherford, Lyndhurst, Moonachie, Rutherford, Teterboro and Wood-Ridge. By the agency's count its Berry's Creek Study Area takes in some six miles of waterway and roughly 750 acres of marsh, and in 2018 it chose an interim cleanup plan estimated at $332 million, with a final decision to come later. The study area is part of the Ventron/Velsicol Superfund site, where, the EPA says, a mercury processing plant ran in Wood-Ridge and Carlstadt from 1929 until 1974; as read in November 2026, the EPA's site page says the agency is preparing to clean up Berry's Creek. [@epa-berrys-creek-2018] [@epa-ventron]
