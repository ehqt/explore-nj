New Jersey has 564 municipalities, a figure given by both the state's official website and the New Jersey State League of Municipalities. [@nj-fast-facts] [@njlm-forms]

In its 2010 guide to New Jersey's geography, the Census Bureau said all areas of the state "have a township government except areas within an incorporated place," meaning a borough, city, town or village. A current Census Bureau glossary names New Jersey as one of 12 states whose minor civil divisions (in New Jersey, the townships) act as full local governments, able to do the same jobs as incorporated places. [@census-nj-guide-2010] [@census-glossary]

The League sorts every municipality into one of five types: borough, township, city, town and village. [@njlm-forms]

The League notes that New Jersey's municipalities "vary widely in size and needs." In the Census Bureau's 2010 figures, the most densely populated incorporated place in the state was Guttenberg, a town. That year the township with the most people, Edison, had 99,967 residents, and the township with the most land, Hamilton Township in Atlantic County, covered 111.13 square miles. [@njlm-forms] [@census-nj-guide-2010]

Type and form of government are also separate things. According to the League, all 564 municipalities use one of 12 forms of government. Five of those forms grew up historically alongside the five types; the other seven are optional forms that municipalities can adopt, one of them open only to places with fewer than 12,000 people. The League notes that bigger municipalities tend to pick a form under the Faulkner Act, and says there is no "best" form. [@njlm-forms]

According to the League, the system's roots reach back to colonial times: sitting midway along the 13 colonies, New Jersey blended local-government ideas from the colonies on either side. [@njlm-forms]

Bergen County's government tells one part of the story in an overview of the county's history. It says a late-19th-century change in state law made it easy to form boroughs, setting off what it calls "the Boroughitis phenomenon." By the overview's account, an 1894 school act made each township its own school district but exempted boroughs, towns, villages and cities from paying a share of the old districts' debts, and "the rush was on to create new boroughs." Between January 23 and December 18, 1894, 26 boroughs were carved out of Bergen's townships, and new municipalities kept forming in the county until 1924, when there were 70. [@bergen-overview]

## Why it matters

The "city" in a mailing address is a post office name tied to the ZIP Code. The Census Bureau notes that this "city" name "may differ from the legal municipality" where a home actually sits, and that it may not match the name residents think of as home. The Bureau itself assigns each home by its physical location, not its postal city. [@census-usps-city]

Some local place names are not municipalities. The Census Bureau publishes figures for "census designated places," named communities that are not legally incorporated. Their boundaries carry no legal status, and they have no officials "elected to serve traditional municipal functions." In 2010 it counted 221 of them in New Jersey; the largest by population was the Toms River CDP, a statistical area, with 88,791 people. The state's Locality Search on NJ.gov lists New Jersey local names alongside their municipality and county. [@census-glossary] [@census-nj-guide-2010] [@nj-locality-search]
