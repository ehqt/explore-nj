At a New Jersey "jughandle," the left lane is not where a left turn starts. The state transportation department's design manual defines a jughandle as an "at-grade ramp" at or between intersections that lets drivers make "indirect left turns and/or U-turns." Drivers peel off to the right, either before reaching the cross street or just after it, and the ramp brings them back to a signal where they cross the highway. New Jersey Monthly summed it up as a design that makes drivers "turn right in order to turn left." [@njdot-rdm] [@njm-2014]

Federal researchers describe several layouts. With a "forward" ramp, anyone turning, right or left, exits to the right before the intersection; left-turners then cross at the cross street, and someone wanting to head back the other way turns left there instead. With a "reverse" ramp, left-turners first ride through the intersection, then take a loop ramp off the right lane beyond it. [@fhwa-techbrief]

The name is literal. A 1960 State Highway Department brochure explained that, viewed from the air, the basic ramp looks like the handle on an earthen jug. The brochure presented jughandles as a safety measure for heavily used highways, since they take turns out of lanes carrying moving traffic and cut the delays those turns cause. Its summary for 1954 through August 1960 counted 233 of them completed or under way. [@shd-1960]

Exactly when the first one appeared is unclear. In 2013 a state transportation spokesman told WHYY they date to the 1930s or '40s, and New Jersey Monthly says the design debuted in the state's roadway plans in the late 1930s and '40s. A 2013 CBS and Associated Press report said the state does not know when the first was built or exactly how many it has. [@whyy-2013] [@njm-2014] [@cbs-2013]

Do they work? A 2007 Federal Highway Administration research brief reported that, in computer simulations of typical designs, jughandle intersections had lower average delays and more capacity than ordinary intersections when traffic was close to saturated, and performed about the same in lighter traffic, where travel times and stops were comparable or slightly higher. It also summarized a study of a limited sample of 44 jughandle and 50 conventional intersections that found fatal-plus-injury crashes per million entering vehicles roughly 26 percent lower at the jughandles, though the jughandles saw relatively more rear-end crashes. Not every study agreed: the same brief cites another simulation study suggesting jughandles do no better than conventional designs on average travel time. [@fhwa-techbrief]

## Why it matters

Jughandles are commonly described as quintessentially New Jersey. A 2013 CBS and Associated Press report called them "as New Jersey as diners and Bruce Springsteen," and New Jersey Monthly said in 2014 that the design is "as much a part of our state as Taylor ham and Bruce Springsteen." The Federal Highway Administration brief calls them "New Jersey jughandle intersections." In the 2014 article, the state transportation department's acting communications director put the count at more than 600, which he said was the most of any state. [@cbs-2013] [@njm-2014] [@fhwa-techbrief]

They can take some getting used to. The same magazine noted that novice drivers and out-of-staters "can be baffled," and a Rutgers transportation safety engineer it interviewed said they often sit on corner lots that could otherwise be developed. In 2013 Republican State Senator James W. Holzapfel proposed barring any new jughandles while leaving existing ones alone; the bill cleared a Senate committee, but New Jersey Monthly reported in 2014 that it had failed. [@njm-2014] [@cbs-2013] [@whyy-2013]

There is one more New Jersey driving rule worth knowing, at the gas station. Under the state's Retail Gasoline Dispensing Safety Act, an attendant may not let anyone who is not an attendant put fuel into a vehicle, and the law's findings cite the fire hazards of dispensing fuel. CNN reported in 2023 that, after Oregon lifted its ban, New Jersey became the only state where drivers cannot pump their own gas, and NBC10 Philadelphia said the same in 2025; both date New Jersey's ban to 1949. [@nj-gas-act] [@cnn-2023] [@nbc10-2025]
