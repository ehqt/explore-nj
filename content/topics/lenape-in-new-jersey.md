People lived in what is now New Jersey for thousands of years before Europeans came, and New Jersey's state archaeologist says archaeological sites statewide show people living here as far back as 12,000 years. The Lenape homeland is spelled Lenapehöking in the state archaeologist's presentation and Lënapehòkink by Liberty Hall Museum. When Europeans arrived, the Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape Tribal Nation says, its Lenape ancestors were the people of New Jersey, Delaware, eastern Pennsylvania and southern New York. [@njdoe-lattanzi] [@lh-land] [@nll-about]

The tribal nation translates Lenni-Lenape as "Original People" and names three dialect groups: the Munsee in the north, and the Unami and Unalachtigo in the central and southern parts of the homeland. In its own language, the Delaware Tribe of Indians says, Lenape means "something like 'The People.'" Europeans called them Delaware, after the river. [@nll-about] [@delaware-tribe-about]

Colonization pushed most Lenape out. The state archaeologist's presentation dates the start of the Lenape "being forced west" to the beginning of the 1700s. The Historical Commission plan describes the 1778 Treaty of Fort Pitt as the young country's first treaty with the Lenape. Afterward, the Delaware Tribe says, its ancestors kept losing land and moving west, through Ohio, Indiana, Missouri and Kansas, until they were forced into Indian Territory, now Oklahoma; small groups fled to Canada. The Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape say many of their relatives "suffered removals and forced migrations." [@njdoe-lattanzi] [@njhc-plan] [@delaware-tribe-about] [@delaware-tribe-faq] [@nll-about]

Some Lenape stayed. In 1758 the colony set up the Brotherton reservation at what is now Indian Mills, in Shamong Township, Burlington County. The Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape Tribal Nation calls it "the first and only Indian Reservation in New Jersey" and dates it 1758 to 1802; it says the reservation was meant as "a safe haven" for some of its ancestors but, like others, failed to protect them. Accounts of the settlement differ: the Indian Mills Historical Society describes "a self-supporting community," while the state archaeologist's presentation says Brotherton "was not self-sufficient." In 1801 its residents accepted an invitation to move north to New York; the historical society names the Oneidas as the hosts, and the presentation names a "New Stockbridge" group. [@pinelands-history] [@imhs-sites] [@nll-about] [@nll-history] [@njdoe-lattanzi]

As of 2026, federally recognized Lenape nations include the Delaware Tribe of Indians, based in Bartlesville, Oklahoma, and the Delaware Nation, at Anadarko, Oklahoma, whose aboriginal name, it says, is "Lenape or Lenni-Lenape." The state archaeologist also lists the Stockbridge-Munsee Community, which the Historical Commission plan places in Wisconsin, and two First Nations in Canada. [@delaware-tribe-about] [@delaware-nation-about] [@njhc-plan] [@njdoe-lattanzi]

As of November 2026, the state's Commission on American Indian Affairs gives two seats to each of the three tribal nations the state recognizes. The Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape Tribal Nation describes its citizens as descendants of Nanticoke and Lenape people who "remained, or returned" to the homeland; it says its Nanticoke ancestors came north from Delaware and Maryland's Eastern Shore and joined the Lenape who had remained here. Its citizens elect a nine-member Tribal Council. For the Ramapough Lenape Nation, as the state's records name it, the Ramapough Culture and Land Foundation, a nonprofit whose board president it identifies as Vincent Mann, Turtle Clan Chief of the Ramapough Lenape Nation, describes the Ramapough as Munsee-speaking people who "stayed behind" when others were forced to move, and describes the Turtle Clan's community in Ringwood. A website titled Ramapo Munsee Lenape Network, which described itself as a state-recognized tribal nation (archived November 2025; no longer online), said Ramapo means "Sweet Water" and that its people "barely" controlled their ancestral home, and thanked the Hudson River Presbytery for returning land and property. The Powhatan Renape Nation, whose website teaches about "Powhatan people and culture," says it lost its home at the former Rankokus Reservation, now Rancocas State Park, and has moved while keeping its traditions: "we as Indigenous people are still here." [@nll-about] [@nll-history] [@rclf-who] [@ramapough-home] [@powhatan-home] [@oag-2019] [@njcaia-about]

State recognition has been disputed. A 2023 plan for the New Jersey Historical Commission says all three gained it in 1980, though the state's 2018 settlement dates its recognition of the Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape to 1982. In 2015 the Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape sued the state, alleging that information from the state had led the U.S. General Accounting Office to be told New Jersey recognized no Native American tribes, and that this had cost it grants, contracts and the right to sell crafts as "American Indian-made." In a November 2018 settlement, the state acknowledged that recognition and agreed to pay it $2.4 million, without admitting wrongdoing. In 2019 the state settled with the Powhatan Renape and Ramapough Lenape nations, neither of which had sued, acknowledging their recognition since 1980 and revoking any past denials. The tribes disclaimed any interest in casino gaming rights, and the settlements state that recognition gives none of the three federal casino gaming rights. [@njhc-plan] [@oag-2018] [@oag-2019]

## Why it matters

Lenape history in New Jersey did not end with removal. The Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape describe their past as including "a homeland presence that never ended," and Liberty Hall Museum notes that although many were forced west, "some remained in the state and continue to live in the area today." A 2023 plan for the New Jersey Historical Commission found that "up-to-date, comprehensive histories of all New Jersey Nations and Tribes" are needed, especially for K–12 education.
