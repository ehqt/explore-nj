The events in Newark in July 1967 go by more than one name. The state and federal commissions whose reports came out in 1968 wrote of "civil disorders" and "the Newark riot." Amiri Baraka, the poet and playwright, who was present, called it a rebellion rather than a riot, and in a 2017 WNYC profile the lawyer and activist Junius Williams called it "the Rebellion." The Newark Public Library named its 50th-anniversary exhibit "Voices from the Rebellion." In 2017 Newark's mayor, Ras Baraka, Amiri Baraka's son, said the language people use for those days shows "how far off people are in their interpretation of the events." [@lilley-report] [@kerner-report] [@npr-baraka] [@npr-hill] [@wnyc-williams] [@npl-voices]

By 1967 Newark had a Black majority but, in NPR's words, "a white-dominated government." Black residents fought the city's offer of 150 acres of the Central Ward for a new state medical and dental college, and many in the Black community felt ignored when Mayor Hugh Addonizio's administration offered a powerful school board post to Councilman James T. Callaghan, who was white, rather than to budget director Wilbur Parker, who was Black. [@npr-baraka] [@kerner-report] [@lilley-report]

On the night of Wednesday, July 12, two white Newark police officers, John DeSimone and Vito Pontrelli, stopped and arrested John W. Smith, a Black cab driver and Army veteran, 40, born in Georgia. People in the Hayes Homes, a housing project facing the Fourth Precinct station, saw him dragged inside; the Kerner Commission says Smith "either refused or was unable to walk." Before the Lilley Commission, the officers testified that Smith hit them and fought them in the patrol car. Smith testified that police punched and kicked him in the car and in the station, and that hospital doctors found his ribs caved in. The commission called the conflicts "glaring" and did not resolve them; NPR's 2017 account says Smith was arrested and beaten. [@riseup-spark] [@kerner-report] [@lilley-report] [@npr-hill]

A false rumor that Smith had died spread, and a crowd gathered. Molotov cocktails hit the station wall; civil rights leaders then tried to lead the crowd on a march to City Hall, but rocks flew from the housing project grounds, and a few stores were looted. On Thursday evening a "Police Brutality Protest Rally" there ended in rock throwing, and looting and fires spread along Springfield Avenue. [@npr-baraka] [@kerner-report] [@lilley-report]

Before dawn on Friday, July 14, the mayor sought State Police and National Guard help from Governor Richard J. Hughes; about 600 troopers, almost half the State Police, were committed that day, the Guard's numbers passed 5,000 by Monday, and on Saturday a cordon sealed off 14 square miles. [@kerner-report] [@lilley-report]

At about six on Saturday evening, Guardsmen and state troopers, believing snipers were there, fired heavily into the Hayes Homes; Eloise Spellman, who had several children, was killed in her 10th-floor apartment, and two other women nearby, Hattie Gainer and Rebecca Brown, were also killed. Ten-year-old Eddie Moss was shot dead in his family's car after it swerved around a National Guard roadblock. The Kerner Commission said snipers were blamed for the deaths of a detective, Fred Toto, and a fire captain, Michael Moran, though it could not trace the shot that killed Toto. Police Director Dominick Spina told the commission, "I really don't believe there was as much sniping as we thought." [@kerner-report] [@lilley-report]

The Lilley Commission counted 26 deaths connected with the disorders: 23 from gunshot wounds and three from other causes, a heart attack, a narcotics overdose and a car crash with a fire engine. The Kerner Commission's Newark chapter gives a lower figure, 23 killed. Its list of the dead shows 24 Black people and two white men. State Police records counted 725 people reported injured, though Newark City Hospital's medical director said 1,020 were treated there alone; 1,465 people were arrested. According to NPR's 2017 report, no charges were ever filed over any of the deaths. [@lilley-report] [@kerner-report] [@npr-hill]

On Sunday, Black leaders began calling for the troops to leave, feeling, as Spina put it, that their presence had "created and intensified the unrest." Early on Monday, July 17, Governor Hughes decided to pull them out except for a skeleton crew; they began leaving that afternoon, and by Tuesday all but a small force had gone. Sources give the length of the unrest as four, five or six days. [@lilley-report] [@kerner-report] [@npr-hill] [@npr-baraka] [@aclu-nj-50] [@wnyc-williams]

## Why it matters

New Jersey's Governor's Select Commission on Civil Disorder, known as the Lilley Commission, reported in February 1968 that no single factor could explain the violence. It pointed instead to evidence of worsening ghetto conditions, residents' growing frustration with them, the rise of outspoken groups that focused that anger, and "miscalculations, insensitive or inadequate responses by established authority." It found "virtually a complete breakdown" in relations between the police and the Black community before July. During the riot, it said, there was evidence of prejudice against Black people among some police and Guard units, which led to "excessive and unjustified force." Testimony "strongly suggests," it said, that State Police, with some Guardsmen, were mainly responsible for damaging stores marked with "Soul" signs to show non-white ownership, which it said had "no possible justification." It found no support for the idea of a conspiracy or plan to start the riot, though it allowed that some sniping may have been organized once the riot peaked on Friday. [@lilley-report] [@rutgers-investigations]

The federal Kerner Commission, appointed by President Lyndon Johnson, studied the 1967 disorders in Newark and other cities. It concluded that the nation was "moving toward two societies, one black, one white—separate and unequal." It found that "white racism is essentially responsible" for the "explosive mixture" that had been building in American cities since World War II. The findings were disputed: a 1968 study by the New Jersey State Patrolman's Benevolent Association called the conclusions of both commissions "unsubstantiated, unfounded and unwarranted." [@kerner-report] [@rutgers-investigations]

In 1970 Newark elected Kenneth Gibson, its first Black mayor. The ACLU of New Jersey says Newarkers first called for federal oversight of their police in response to abuses exposed in 1967; in 2016, nearly 50 years on, the U.S. Justice Department and the Newark police entered a consent decree meant to stop civil rights abuses. [@npr-hill] [@rutgers-mayors] [@aclu-nj-50]

The 50th anniversary in 2017 brought a Rutgers University–Newark conference on Black urban history since 1967, the Newark Public Library's exhibit "Voices from the Rebellion," an interfaith service at Abyssinian Baptist Church, and a real-time retelling of the events on Twitter by the ACLU of New Jersey.
