Ask for a breakfast sandwich almost anywhere in New Jersey and you'll meet a smoked, spiced pork product that is sliced, seared and served with egg and cheese on a hard roll or bagel. It is sold in diners, delis and bagel shops across the state. What to call it is the question. [@eater-2015] [@bill-a3666]

The names trace back to Taylor Provisions of Trenton, which sold the product in the late 1800s as "Taylor's Prepared Ham." After the federal Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906, the label had to change because, as a federal court put it in 1910, the product "did not consist of ham." It was renamed "Taylor's Pork Roll." [@court-1910] [@bill-a3667] [@taylor-history]

The split is commonly described as geographic: North Jersey residents call it "Taylor ham" and South Jersey residents call it "pork roll," and breakfast menus follow suit, listing "pork roll" in Central and South Jersey and "Taylor ham" in the north. [@eater-2015] [@bill-a3667]

Its origins are told two ways too. The 2016 Assembly bills describe as "New Jersey folklore" the story that the meat was carried as field rations by Continental Army soldiers at the Battle of Trenton, while the Taylor company's own history says Colonel John Taylor supplied "Taylor" minced ham to Washington's troops there. [@bill-a3666] [@bill-a3667] [@taylor-history]

## Why it matters

The name is commonly described as one way to tell whether someone is from North or South Jersey, and the debate over this "beloved cured meat," as the bills call it, is ongoing. [@eater-2015] [@bill-a3666] [@bill-a3667]

On April 14, 2016, Assemblyman Tim Eustace introduced two bills to make the sandwich the official State Sandwich: one called it "pork roll, egg, and cheese," and the other "Taylor Ham, egg, and cheese." [@bill-a3666] [@bill-a3667]
