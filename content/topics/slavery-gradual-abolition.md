Slavery existed in New Jersey from the colonial period, and it ended slowly. In 1804 the state passed a law for the gradual abolition of slavery. It freed no one who was already enslaved. Instead, children born to enslaved mothers after July 4, 1804, were to be freed only after serving their mother's enslaver as "apprentices," until age 21 for women and 25 for men. The change was slow: 7,557 people were still enslaved in New Jersey in 1820. [@njsl-antebellum] [@princeton-slavery]

In 1846 a new law declared slavery abolished, but it made every person still enslaved an "apprentice" for life, bound to the person who held them, which the New Jersey State Library describes as a modified form of slavery. Children later born to these "apprentices" were free from birth. According to the State Library, nearly 700 people were still enslaved in New Jersey when that law passed, most of them over 55. The census counted 236 in 1850, and the 1860 federal census still listed eighteen. [@njsl-antebellum] [@princeton-slavery]

In 1865, New Jersey voted against ratifying the Thirteenth Amendment, which abolished slavery nationwide. The amendment took effect that December without New Jersey's approval. [@princeton-slavery] [@njsl-civil-war]

## Why it matters

New Jersey became a "free state" with its 1804 law, but slavery lingered here into the 1860s. The state was also a key route on the Underground Railroad's eastern corridor, between Pennsylvania and New York, with stations in towns such as Salem, Camden, Burlington, Trenton, Princeton, New Brunswick, Newark and Jersey City. Black communities such as Lawnside in Camden County and Timbuctoo in Burlington County became havens for people escaping slavery, and William Still, one of the Underground Railroad's best-known organizers, was born in New Jersey. [@njsl-antebellum]
