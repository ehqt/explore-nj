New Jersey is commonly called the "Diner Capital of the World." A joint resolution introduced in the state Senate in January 2022 opens with that phrase, and a 2021 National Register nomination uses a close cousin, "Diner Capital of America," for the crowded field of New Jersey diner builders in 1932. [@sjr32] [@aetna-nomination]

The 2022 resolution counted roughly 525 diners in New Jersey, which it called "the largest number of diners in any state." A 2010 Smithsonian Magazine article gave a higher count, "600-plus," and named New Jersey the most "diner-supplied" state. The two figures come from different years. [@sjr32] [@smithsonian-2010]

In the traditional sense, a diner is a building made in a factory and then transported to the spot where it serves food, which the Smithsonian article gives as one difference between a diner and a coffee shop. The word "diner" itself appears to have replaced older terms like lunch wagon and lunch car around 1923 or 1924. [@smithsonian-2010] [@skees-nomination]

New Jersey was a center of that factory trade, though sources count its builders differently. Preservation New Jersey said in 2010 that the state had "at least six and as many as 20" diner makers between the 1920s and the 1980s. A 2015 National Trust for Historic Preservation article put northern New Jersey's total at "more than a dozen" by the 1950s. The Aetna Diner nomination says Paramount faced twenty other New Jersey manufacturers when it started out in 1932. [@pnj-2010] [@ntrust-2015] [@aetna-nomination]

Start with Jerry O'Mahony, Inc. A 2002 National Register nomination says Jeremiah Mahoney and his partner, John J. Hanf, set up shop in a Bayonne garage in 1913 and built two lunch cars there. The company moved into a new plant in Elizabeth around 1925, had its busiest year in 1928 with 184 diners, and made its last diner in 1955. The 2022 resolution tells the start a little differently: Jerry O'Mahony and his brother owned horse-drawn lunch wagons in Hudson County, then hired a carpenter to build a food wagon, which they sold. [@skees-nomination] [@sjr32]

Kullman began making diners in 1927, after Samuel Kullman left his job as an accountant at the diner builder P. J. Tierney, according to a 2002 nomination for a Rhode Island diner. That diner was built in 1947 by the Kullman Dining Car Company of Harrison, New Jersey, and the nomination describes Kullman as "one of the largest and longest-lived diner manufacturers in the U.S." [@poirier-nomination]

The National Trust reports that Silk City diners came out of Paterson from 1927 to 1964, made by the Paterson Vehicle Company, and that the factory's spot beside a railroad let finished diners travel across the country. Preservation New Jersey also lists Silk City among the state's best-known makers, alongside O'Mahony of Elizabeth and the Fodero Dining Car Company of Bloomfield. [@ntrust-2015] [@pnj-2010]

Arthur E. Sieber founded Paramount Dining Cars in New Jersey in 1932, according to the Aetna Diner nomination, and its Haledon factory built that Hartford diner in 1947. In 1941 he received a patent for a "Portable Diner" split lengthwise into separate pieces that were put together on site. Until then, the nomination explains, a diner's width was capped by what one railroad car could carry. The nomination, citing Richard Gutman's book American Diner: Then and Now, also credits Paramount with making stainless steel popular inside diners. [@aetna-nomination]

Those factories sent their work well beyond the state. National Register nominations for diners in Torrington and Hartford, Connecticut, and Providence, Rhode Island, trace each one to a New Jersey maker: O'Mahony, Paramount of Haledon and Kullman of Harrison. [@skees-nomination] [@aetna-nomination] [@poirier-nomination]

## Why it matters

Preservation New Jersey noted in 2010 that because so many builders were nearby, getting a diner to its site in this part of the country was inexpensive and simple. It added that the state's busy highways, running between big cities, suburbs and the Shore, suited roadside businesses, diners among them. [@pnj-2010]

The 2022 resolution links diners to the state's place between New York City and Philadelphia: commuters crossing New Jersey stopped at its 24-hour diners to eat and rest, and for many working-class and middle-class families, a diner was a place where everyone could eat without spending much. It proposed making October 1 "New Jersey Diner Day" each year. [@sjr32]

Gutman, whom Smithsonian Magazine called a "diner scholar," describes the crowd as mixed. "In diners, you get people from all walks of life, a real cross-section," he told Smithsonian Magazine in 2010. [@smithsonian-2010]

Their survival is not guaranteed. When Preservation New Jersey put the state's historic diners on its endangered list in 2010, it pointed to competition from national chain restaurants, changing eating habits, teardowns, and rising land values that made moving diners away from their original sites common. [@pnj-2010]
