Whitesbog is a small farm village on sandy roads in Pemberton Township, ringed by pine forest, reservoirs, blueberry fields and cranberry bogs. The Whitesbog Preservation Trust, the nonprofit that looks after the village, counts about 3,000 acres around it and gives 1857 as the village's founding year. According to its National Register nomination (prepared in 1982), the State of New Jersey bought the 3,000-acre tract from the J.J. White Company in 1967 as an addition to Lebanon State Forest; the Trust now describes Whitesbog as part of Brendan T. Byrne State Forest. [@wpt-home] [@wpt-history] [@wpt-faq] [@nr-1982]

Cranberries came first. The nomination says Colonel James A. Fenwick bought 108 acres along Cranberry Run in 1857 to grow them, south of Hanover Furnace, where bog iron had once been processed. After Fenwick died in 1882, his son-in-law, Joseph J. White, took over management of the farm; the Trust says the property itself remained with Fenwick's widow until her death in 1911. White expanded the holdings to roughly 3,000 acres over the next three decades. The nomination says Whitesbog was New Jersey's largest cranberry farm by 1912, and the Trust also calls it the state's largest in the early 1900s. The Trust says the fifth generation of the family farms Whitesbog today. [@nr-1982] [@wpt-history] [@wpt-about]

Blueberries came next. Elizabeth Coleman White, the eldest of J.J. White's daughters, had joined the family business in 1893. Experiments in Blueberry Culture, a U.S. Department of Agriculture bulletin by the botanist Frederick V. Coville, caught the Whites' interest, and in 1911 they agreed to back his research, hoping blueberries could give the farm a crop to grow alongside cranberries. Local woodsmen were paid to scout the woods within about 20 miles of Whitesbog for wild bushes worth breeding from, and the partners ran years of propagation and cross-breeding trials; New Jersey Women's History says Coville did the crossing by hand. By 1916, the partners had blueberries to sell. [@nr-1982] [@njwh-white] [@ars-2011]

## Why it matters

Whitesbog's blueberry work is written into state law. The act naming the highbush blueberry New Jersey's state fruit, approved January 12, 2004, credits White and Coville's work at Whitesbog in the early 1900s with the fruit's first cultivation "for commercial production," and says growing highbush blueberries in the state became the basis for "an entirely new agricultural industry." New Jersey Women's History likewise says the pair produced the first commercial crop of blueberries by 1916, and the Trust calls Whitesbog the "Birthplace of the Blueberry industry." [@nj-law-2004] [@njwh-white] [@wpt-home]

Elizabeth White is also a figure in women's history. She helped organize the New Jersey Blueberry Cooperative Association in 1927, and both the nomination and New Jersey Women's History name her as the first woman member of the American Cranberry Association. The Trust describes Whitesbog as "a significant Women's History destination." [@nr-1982] [@njwh-white] [@wpt-home]

Migrant workers harvested the cranberries, and the nomination points to Whitesbog's "well-documented role in the social history of Italian migrant workers in America." Those who came each September and October for the harvest lived in two nearby settlements, Florence and Rome, which the state demolished in the 1970s. [@nr-1982]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The village trails are open every day as part of the state forest, and the Trust runs tours, hikes and seasonal events, including events in blueberry season and walks to watch the cranberry harvest from a distance (visitors may not go into the bogs). Tours of Elizabeth White's house, Suningive, and her Pinelands Native Garden are also offered. Check the official site for hours and current programs. [@wpt-faq] [@wpt-home]
