Island Beach State Park covers a thin barrier island that runs 10 miles between the Atlantic Ocean and Barnegat Bay, which lies on its west side. The New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection, in its park page as archived in October 2026, describes the island as "shaped by storms and tides." By the department's account, more than 3,000 acres and 10 miles of coastal dunes here "remain almost untouched" since 1609, when Henry Hudson described the New Jersey coast from his ship, the Half Moon. [@dep-park]

According to the park's nature program, Henry Phipps, whom it describes as Andrew Carnegie's partner in Pittsburgh steel, bought the island in 1926 and built three model homes for a planned exclusive summer resort, a plan the 1929 crash and the Depression brought to an end. Fishermen and hunters already had shacks on the land, and the Friends of the Judge's Shack say Phipps let them stay if they paid a yearly fee to lease their spot. The nature program says that once Phipps died in 1930, the estate's foreman, Francis Freeman, watched over the land, and that Freeman, his wife and a retired Coast Guard captain, Joseph Tilton, formed the Borough of Island Beach in 1933. Freeman's rules were simple, ending with "don't annoy the osprey." [@ibnp-history] [@judges-shack]

The nature program says Johns Hopkins University scientists used the island in 1945 to test what it calls "the world's first supersonic anti-aircraft missile," in a project called Operation Bumblebee. A committee formed that year tried to raise money so the National Park Service could buy the island, but the funding never came. New Jersey bought it in 1953, for $2.7 million according to the nature program or $2.75 million according to the Friends of the Judge's Shack. Accepting the deed, Governor Alfred Driscoll said, "Island Beach is unique. It is a jewel." The park opened to the public in 1959. The state let existing leaseholders keep their shacks, with each lease ending when its holder died; a 2019 article by the Friends of the Judge's Shack counted six lease sites left as of 2018, down from well over 100 in the 1920s. [@ibnp-history] [@judges-shack] [@friends-guide]

## Why it matters

The DEP's park page, as archived in October 2026, calls the park "one of New Jersey's last significant remnants of a barrier island ecosystem" that once ran along much of the coast, and "one of the few remaining undeveloped barrier beaches on the north Atlantic coast." The same page describes Island Beach's rolling dunes, green salt marshes and nine plant communities, including "a nationally significant maritime forest." More than 400 plants have been identified here, and the page says the park has New Jersey's largest expanses of beach heather. [@dep-park]

Volunteers have helped build the dunes. American beachgrass catches blowing sand and keeps growing up through it, which helps dunes build; the dunes in turn soak up wave energy in storms and hold sand that refills eroded beaches. A Barnegat Bay Partnership post dated December 8, 2017, describes one planting, sponsored by the Friends of Island Beach State Park, a nonprofit volunteer group incorporated in 1996: 335 volunteers set out 30,000 beachgrass plants on a December day. [@bbp-dunes] [@friends-about]

The same DEP page describes the Sedge Islands Marine Conservation Zone as about 1,600 acres of tidal marshes, creeks, ponds and open water, reached by canoe or kayak. It says the zone is meant to protect and interpret wildlife while keeping the area's traditional uses; jet skis and commercial activities are banned. Visitors on guided tours there can see what the page calls New Jersey's largest osprey colony. [@dep-park]

Ospreys across New Jersey dropped to fewer than 60 nesting pairs in the 1970s because of pesticide use and habitat loss, and numbered more than 800 pairs in 2025, according to the Barnegat Bay Partnership. In the summer of 2025 nests at Sedge Island did less well than in earlier years, which the partnership says may be linked to signs of fewer Atlantic menhaden in the bay, one of the birds' most important foods in the region. Piping plovers, shorebirds whose Atlantic Coast population was listed as federally threatened in 1985, are described on the DEP page as occasional inhabitants of the park's open beaches and dunes. [@bbp-osprey] [@usfws-plover] [@dep-park]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. As archived in October 2026, the DEP's page lists eight trails, each under a mile, along with 10 miles of beach, summer guided kayak tours through the Sedge Islands, and nature programs at the Forked River Interpretive and Nature Center, 7 miles into the park. When the swimming areas fill, usually on weekends and holidays, the park stops letting in more visitors until parking opens up. Check the official site for current hours, fees and conditions. [@dep-park]
