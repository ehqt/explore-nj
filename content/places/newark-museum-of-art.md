The Newark Museum of Art sits at 49 Washington Street in downtown Newark, across Washington Park from Broad Street. John Cotton Dana, who also led the Newark Public Library, established it in 1909, and the museum still describes its founding idea as "radical among museums in 1909": that art and learning are for everyone. [@nmoa-visit] [@nmoa-dana] [@nmoa-about]

The museum began inside the library. In 1909 it occupied the fourth floor of the library's building on Washington Street, according to the library's history, which also notes that Dana led the library until he died in 1929. A 1978 National Register nomination for the surrounding historic district records that the museum's own three-story building was completed in 1926, paid for with a $750,000 gift from Louis Bamberger. [@npl-history] [@nr-james-street]

The museum says it cares for "more than 300,000 artworks and artifacts in science and natural history." The very first purchase was several thousand Japanese works from a Newark pharmacist, George T. Rockwell, and the Asian holdings have grown to nearly 30,000 objects, including a Tibetan collection the museum calls "the largest in North America." Its pages also list more than 12,000 works of American art, about 7,000 objects of African art collected since 1917, and roughly 170,000 science objects, from seashells and minerals to a mastodon skeleton. [@nmoa-about] [@nmoa-asia] [@nmoa-american] [@nmoa-africa] [@nmoa-science]

## Why it matters

The museum calls itself "New Jersey's largest fine art museum," and a Rutgers University Libraries research guide calls it "the largest museum in New Jersey." The Rutgers guide also credits Dana's philosophy for the museum's "unusually strong service and educational commitment." [@nmoa-about] [@rutgers-guide]

The museum says it was "one of the first museums to welcome children, offer extended hours, and provide multilingual labels." [@nmoa-next-chapter] Its science page quotes Dana's view that a museum should "educate, inspire and transform individuals of all ages." [@nmoa-science]

The museum's campus also takes in the Ballantine House, a brick mansion designed by architect George Edward Harney for Jeannette and John Holme Ballantine of Newark's beer-brewing family. The museum dates it to 1885 and counts 27 rooms; a 1984 National Historic Landmark nomination gives 1884 and says the house was "originally designed to be a spacious seventeen-room" home. Family members lived there until 1920, when it was sold to an insurance company, and the museum bought it in 1937. It became a National Historic Landmark in 1985, and the museum restored it inside and out between 2021 and 2023. [@nmoa-ballantine-space] [@nmoa-ballantine] [@nhl-ballantine] [@nps-nhl-list]

The 1984 landmark nomination noted that most of Newark's fine houses had disappeared and that the Ballantine House was by then the only survivor of the "fine late 19th century town houses" that once stood around Washington Square. It also called the house one of the very few nineteenth-century "palaces" with complete records, down to bills for decorating and furniture. The museum now uses its period rooms to show off its decorative arts collection, calling the house that collection's "largest collection object." [@nhl-ballantine] [@nmoa-decorative]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The galleries, the Ballantine House and the Dreyfuss Planetarium, which the museum describes as New Jersey's first, are all part of one campus. A construction project that began in January 2025 has removed on-site parking, so the museum points visitors to nearby garages, Newark Light Rail and buses. Exhibitions change, so check the official site for current hours, admission and what is on view. [@nmoa-about] [@nmoa-science] [@nmoa-next-chapter] [@nmoa-visit]
