The Peter Mott House is a small, two-story wood-frame house in Lawnside, a Camden County borough of about 3,000 people, according to the 2022 Census Bureau survey figure the borough cites. Peter Mott, a free Black farmer born around 1807, bought the first of three parcels here in 1844. He and his wife, Elizabeth Ann Mott, lived in the house, which went up in two stages, around 1845 and around 1870. Its 1994 National Register nomination called it the oldest known house in the borough. [@nrhp-nomination] [@lawnside-about]

Before it was Lawnside, the Black settlement here was known as Snow Hill. In 1840 Ralph Smith, a white abolitionist from Philadelphia, laid out lots he named "Free Haven" to signal a refuge from slavery, and the lots were sold at low prices as homesites for free Black people, though Black residents had lived in the area long before. Mott founded the Sunday school of the local church, today's Mount Pisgah African Methodist Episcopal Church, in 1847. The community took the name Lawnside in 1907 and voted in its first borough government in 1926. [@nrhp-nomination] [@lawnside-about] [@pmh-learn]

Lawnside's oral tradition holds that the Motts hid people escaping slavery. In a film cited by the nomination, Doris Scott recalled her grandmother saying that Mott drove freedom seekers in his wagon to Quakers in Haddonfield and Moorestown. Part of Mott's land had earlier belonged to Jacob C. White Sr., a Black Philadelphian prominent in the Underground Railroad, who sold Mott a parcel in 1850. Mott may have escaped slavery himself: census takers recorded his birthplace as New Jersey in 1850 and 1860 but as Delaware in 1870, and both the nomination and historian Paul Schopp suggest he fled bondage. The Lawnside Historical Society notes that "no primary-source documentation or contemporaneous account has been found for verification" of the Motts' role. [@nrhp-nomination] [@pmh-learn]

## Why it matters

Because the Underground Railroad depended on secrecy and left few records, the nomination points out, much of its history rests on accounts written or told later, and it treats oral history in the Black community as strong evidence. It describes the Mott House as a rare example of a pre-Civil War Black landowner's home in Snow Hill. The New Jersey State Library's African American history curriculum lists the house among New Jersey sites associated with the Underground Railroad. [@nrhp-nomination] [@njsl-antebellum]

The nomination links the house to the early growth of Lawnside, which it calls "the only ante-bellum African-American community to later become an incorporated municipality" in New Jersey. The borough's own website makes the same claim, and it also describes itself as the "first independent, self-governing African-American community north of the Mason-Dixon Line." [@nrhp-nomination] [@lawnside-about]

The house was almost lost. After its last resident died in 1987 it sat empty, and a developer who bought and subdivided the land planned to demolish it. Neighbors banded together to save it, forming the Lawnside Historical Society, and the developer eventually donated the house to the society, which received the deed in 1992. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1994, and after an eleven-year restoration it opened as a museum on October 13, 2001. [@nrhp-nomination] [@pmh-learn]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The Lawnside Historical Society runs the house as a museum with guides on duty, and larger groups are asked to reserve a tour. It sits at the end of a residential cul-de-sac, so the society asks visitors to park on Moore or Gloucester avenues rather than on Kings Court. The 19th-century building is only partially accessible and cannot accommodate motorized wheelchairs; visitors with limited mobility may take a modified tour. Check the official site for current hours, fees and closures. [@pmh-visit] [@pmh-learn]
