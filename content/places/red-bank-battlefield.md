On the Delaware River in the town of National Park, Red Bank Battlefield Park is what the county calls "Gloucester County's historic park." In 1777 Americans held a fort here on a prominent bluff known as Red Bank. They called it Fort Mercer, after Brigadier General Hugh Mercer, who had been fatally wounded at the Battle of Princeton that January. Its partner, Fort Mifflin, stood on Mud Island on the Pennsylvania side. [@gc-park] [@selig-2019]

The British took Philadelphia on September 26, 1777. With obstructions sunk in the channel and a small fleet of American boats on the water, the two forts kept the Royal Navy from bringing supplies up the river to the occupied city. On October 22, Hessian troops fighting for the British stormed Fort Mercer. The garrison, led by Colonel Christopher Greene of the 1st Rhode Island Regiment, drove them off, and the Hessian commander, Colonel Carl von Donop, was wounded and died a few days later. Counts of the losses differ. The American Battlefield Trust puts Hessian casualties at "nearly fifty percent," against few American losses, while the county's 2019 study reports a loss of 382 officers and men from an attacking force of about 2,300 Hessian grenadiers. [@abt-fort-mercer] [@selig-2019] [@gc-park]

The win bought only weeks. The Americans gave up Fort Mifflin in mid-November, and when a large British column closed in they left Red Bank during the night of November 18–19; on November 20 a rear guard destroyed the remaining works. [@selig-2019] [@abt-fort-mercer]

## Why it matters

The fort's defenders were not all white. A study published by Gloucester County in 2019 found that the two Rhode Island regiments at Red Bank counted "dozens of African-Americans and Native Americans in their ranks," among them Narragansett men. It names 56 men of color who most likely fought here, while noting that no document places them at the battle directly; their presence is inferred from when they enlisted. The study stresses that this was months before Rhode Island voted, in February 1778, to let enslaved Black and Native American men enlist, freeing each man once he passed muster and paying his owner compensation, the law most Americans link to the term "Black Regiment." [@selig-2019]

The park also keeps the home of the family whose farm became the battlefield. Ann and James Whitall, Quakers, set up a 400-acre plantation at Red Bank in 1748, worked by their nine children and indentured servants from Ireland, the Netherlands and Germany. The fort went up just north of their house, which served as a field hospital during the fighting, and Hessian dead were buried just north of it. James Whitall later claimed damages for more than 8,000 fence rails, along with cedar boards, stakes, posts and a destroyed barn, and the county says the family "never forgave" the Americans for what was done to their land. [@gc-whitall] [@abt-whitall] [@selig-2019]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The county keeps the park open all year, with walking trails, picnic pavilions, a playground and views over the Delaware. The Whitall House runs tours and programs on the park's history, and volunteer gardeners tend gardens of plants typical of the eighteenth century. Check the county's site for current hours and tour dates. [@gc-facility] [@gc-park]
