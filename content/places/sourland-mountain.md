Sourland Mountain is a long, wooded ridge in central New Jersey. Its steep eastern end, in Hillsborough and Montgomery townships, looks out over the flatter Piedmont; from there a chain of ridges and hills heads west through Hopewell and the Amwells and comes down to the Delaware River at Lambertville. The Sourland Conservancy describes the 90-square-mile region as holding "the largest contiguous forest in Central New Jersey," a mix of forest, wetlands and grasslands. [@living-sourlands] [@conservancy-home]

The ridge stands up because its rock resists erosion. Most of it is diabase, an igneous rock, along with a sedimentary rock called argillite; the lowlands on either side were worn down into softer shale and mudstone. The diabase began as magma that pushed between buried layers of sediment, thousands of feet down, while the supercontinent Pangea was pulling apart. It cooled there into a vast sheet, called a sill, that also surfaces at the Palisades of the Hudson and at Baldpate and Pennington mountains. [@geology-hike]

According to the Somerset County Park Commission, the county owns the Sourland Mountain Preserve, which takes in part of the region's northeastern "point." The county's web page gives its size as 6,300 acres, while its 2021 brochure says 6,358. The brochure says the name "is believed to be derived from 'sorrel-land,'" after the reddish-brown soils that early German farmers found. [@county-page] [@county-brochure]

## Why it matters

Diabase lies beneath the Sourland ridges and, as a 2017 Conservancy guide puts it, "peppers its surface with boulders." The same guide argues that the stony, poorly drained ground restricts crop farming and development, which helped the region stay wild from colonial times on. [@living-sourlands]

A 2015 geology hike guide by David Harper, published by the Conservancy, explains one of the preserve's boulder fields. The diabase boulders above the parking lot rest on argillite; the guide says they moved down from diabase outcrops higher on the mountain, traveling several hundred to a thousand feet. Ice-age glaciers never reached Sourland Mountain, but during the ice ages its soil stayed frozen year-round; in one explanation, the top layer thawed each summer into mud that slid downhill carrying the boulders. The guide cautions that this "may not be correct for Sourland Mountain." Along the trail to the Devil's Half Acre, many boulders were rounded by weathering rather than by tumbling in streams: water seeping in turns some of their minerals to clay, which swells and makes the surface flake off, fastest at corners and edges. [@geology-hike]

The county points to birds nesting near the edges of their breeding ranges, among them the Summer Tanager, Winter Wren, and both Black-capped and Carolina Chickadees. Harper's 2015 geology guide notes that two World War II pipelines, the Big Inch and Little Inch, built to bring oil from Texas to the East Coast, cross the preserve. [@county-page] [@geology-hike]

The Sourlands also have a long Black history, which the Stoutsburg Sourland African American Museum in Skillman works to tell. Friday Truehart was brought from South Carolina to New Jersey in 1780, an enslaved boy of 13; after gaining his freedom in 1802, he became, in the museum's words, "one of the Sourland Mountain Region's first African American land-owners." In the mid-1800s, African Americans descended from free and enslaved people of the region organized Mt. Zion AME Church. The people it served worked on farms and in households as laborers and servants, and in the region's quarries and peach orchards. Late in the century a peach blight hit the region, and much of the mountain's Black community was forced to leave to find work. [@ssaam-sites] [@ssaam-about]

In 1899 the church moved to Hollow Road, onto land given by Spencer and Corinda True, and it served an African American congregation until 2005. Beverly Mills and Elaine Buck founded the museum there in 2016, and the church was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 2021. In 2022, the museum and the Sourland Conservancy joined to buy the neighboring Reasoner/True House, a five-acre farm that generations of the True family owned until 1994, and which is now part of the museum. [@ssaam-about] [@ssaam-sites]

## Worth a visit?

Yes, for hikers. The county lists hiking, mountain biking and bird watching in the preserve, and its brochure warns that the trails are "steep, rocky, uneven." The Conservancy's list of trail maps marks the Somerset preserve's map at 7.0 miles and notes that Sourland hikes can lead to boulder fields, wetlands and many hills. The county's page, as archived in November 2025, carried a notice about rehabilitating the Maple Flats trail and boardwalk, so check the official site for current trail conditions and directions. [@county-page] [@county-brochure] [@conservancy-resources]
