Duke Farms covers 2,700 acres on the banks of the Raritan River in Hillsborough. It was first the private estate of James Buchanan Duke, the tobacco industrialist whose name Duke University bears, and his daughter, Doris Duke, inherited control of it when he died. The Doris Duke Foundation now operates it as a center for conservation science and sustainability, which it calls "a living lab." [@ddf-center] [@ddf-founder] [@ddcf-2010] [@df-about]

Duke began in 1893 by purchasing a 357-acre farm along the river. According to Audubon and a Duke Farms timeline, he added more land until the estate reached 2,200 acres; the timeline notes that Doris Duke later bought about 385 more acres in 1969. The foundation's 2010 page says Duke began with "the flat farmland of Hillsborough," and the timeline says Duke had so much earth moved that the landscape's topography was completely transformed. He had a reservoir and lakes dug: the 2010 page credits him with nine lakes, while Audubon's 2012 article lists five. Asked how many trees he had planted, Duke answered "Over 2 million," the timeline records. [@audubon-2012] [@df-timeline-2017] [@ddcf-2010]

Doris Duke was born in 1912, J.B. Duke's only child; her father died in October 1925, when she was 12. Her foundation says this estate is where she began to champion environmental conservation "in earnest." In the 1960s she placed marble statues inside the stone walls of the Hay Barn, which a 1915 fire had gutted. She loved orchids, and in 1940 she developed a new hybrid, Phalaenopsis Doris, which was registered at Duke Farms. [@ddf-founder] [@audubon-2012] [@df-hay-barn] [@df-orchid-range]

## Why it matters

Some of J.B. Duke's water engineering is still at work. In 1900 he dammed the Raritan and built a Filter House to clean river water and pump it up to the reservoir. Vista Lake, completed in 1908, was first called Lake 45, because its surface was 45 feet higher than the river's. Between the reservoir and Vista Lake, Duke designed the Great Falls, an engineered waterfall. It is still part of the estate's system of artificial lakes: water typically runs from the reservoir down the falls, which helps aerate the lakes and keep water circulating. [@df-timeline-2017] [@df-great-falls]

Doris Duke died in 1993, at age 80. Her will set Duke Farms aside "to protect endangered species of all kinds, both flora and fauna" and "for agricultural and horticultural purposes, including research." Work to turn the property into an environmental center began in 2008. In May 2012, a new orientation center opened, and nearly 1,000 acres at the heart of the property became open for visitors to explore on their own. It was not the estate's first opening to visitors: the timeline says J.B. Duke opened the grounds in 1899 for greenhouse displays, picnics and skating, then closed them in 1915 because of vandalism. [@ddf-founder] [@audubon-2012] [@df-timeline-2017]

Duke Farms says it works to restore ecosystems in "a fragmented suburban landscape." It says its meadows, grasslands and forests hold 523 plant species and 247 bird species. Bald eagles nest on the property: staff discovered a nest in the fall of 2004, and today a pair is using the same sycamore that held an earlier nest. An Eagle Cam, run with the Conserve Wildlife Foundation of New Jersey, lets viewers watch them raise their young. The campus also reports reaching 100% clean electricity on a net annual basis, with power from its own solar array and battery systems. [@df-impact] [@df-faqs] [@ddf-center] [@df-sustainability]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. Start at the Orientation Center inside the historic Farm Barn, which was built in 1906 to house thoroughbred racehorses. The trails lie across Dukes Parkway West, a former private road. Duke Farms calls the South Gate crossing the safest way over, with crossing guards there giving walkers a green light. The trail network is given as more than 18 miles on the trails page and as 20 miles on the visit page. Sights on the grounds include the Great Falls, the Hay Barn's sculptures and the Orchid Range, which is open whenever the trails are. [@df-visit] [@df-farm-barn] [@df-trails] [@df-great-falls] [@df-hay-barn]

Duke Farms is almost entirely outdoors, and there is no general indoor visitor center. Pets are not allowed, though service animals are, and bikes are welcome on the paved and gravel paths. From April to October, a Saturday visit requires a parking pass reserved in advance. Check the official site for current hours, closures and programs. [@df-guidelines] [@df-faqs] [@df-home]
