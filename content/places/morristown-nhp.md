Morristown National Historical Park preserves the places where General George Washington and the Continental Army spent their winter encampment from December 1779 to June 1780. The National Park Service calls it the coldest winter on record, and says weather historians agree it was the worst winter of the 18th century. The park is made up of four separate areas: Washington's Headquarters (the museum and the Ford Mansion) and Fort Nonsense in the north, and Jockey Hollow and the New Jersey Brigade area in the south. [@nps-home] [@nps-faq] [@nps-places] [@nps-directions]

Jacob Ford Jr., an iron manufacturer, built the Ford Mansion in the early 1770s. In December 1779 his widow, Theodosia, let Washington use it as his headquarters. Theodosia Ford and her four children kept two rooms for themselves, and the rest of the house went to Washington, Martha Washington, his staff officers and servants, and a number of visiting dignitaries. [@nps-ford]

The soldiers camped in the woods of Jockey Hollow. The army was drawn to Henry Wick's farm, most of it forest, because it needed timber for shelter and fuel, and that winter it cut down more than 600 acres of his trees. On one hillside, some 2,000 Pennsylvania troops lived in lines of log huts. The huts there now are 1960s reconstructions put up by volunteers. [@nps-jockey] [@nps-pa-huts]

## Why it matters

New Jersey saw a great deal of the Revolutionary War. The National Park Service says more than 200 battles and skirmishes were fought in the state, more than anywhere else in the 13 original states, and that Washington spent more time here than anywhere else during the war. A national heritage area that promotes the state's Revolutionary-era sites is named Crossroads of the American Revolution. [@nps-crossroads]

The army camped in the Morristown area several times. After the American victories at Trenton and Princeton, Washington's troops arrived there on January 6, 1777. The park explains why the spot was chosen: it was two days' march from the main British base in New York City, the Watchung Mountains and the Great Swamp protected it from a surprise attack, and its roads let the army move in any direction. For much of the war it also served as a military supply depot. [@nps-fort-nonsense] [@nps-faq] [@nps-crossroads]

The Park Service says that at Morristown the Continental Army "emerged as a cohesive and disciplined fighting force." It estimates that about one hundred soldiers died during the 1779–80 encampment, far fewer than at Valley Forge, and it credits the difference to experience: by then most of the men were veterans who had learned to set up a sound winter camp, and most had been inoculated against smallpox. [@nps-history] [@nps-faq]

The Ford Mansion was put up for public auction in 1873. Four men bought it and then formed the Washington Association of New Jersey to preserve it. The Park Service calls it one of the earliest house museums in the United States. On March 3, 1933, Herbert Hoover signed legislation for the government to accept the house along with land at Jockey Hollow and Fort Nonsense. The deeds were handed over at a ceremony on July 4, 1933, forming what the Park Service describes as the first national historical park. [@nps-ford] [@nps-auction]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The two main areas, and the best places to start, are Washington's Headquarters Museum with the Ford Mansion, and the Jockey Hollow Visitor Center with the Wick House. Rangers there can point you to the park's other areas. The Ford Mansion can be seen only on a guided tour that starts in the museum, and the Jockey Hollow Visitor Center has a large mural of the encampment and a full-scale model of a soldier's cabin. Check the park's official site for current hours and tours. [@nps-directions] [@nps-ford] [@nps-jockey]
