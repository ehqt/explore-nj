Batsto Village is in Wharton State Forest, in the Pine Barrens of Burlington County. Its ironworks dates to 1766, and the Batsto Citizens Committee credits Charles Read with founding it on the Batsto River. The spot had what iron making needed: bog ore dug from the banks of streams, forest wood to burn into charcoal, and water for power. The ironworks turned out household goods such as cooking pots and kettles, and the 1970 National Register nomination lists "pigs," water pipe and stoves among its products. During the Revolutionary War it supplied George Washington's Continental Army; the state parks department's page, as archived in June 2026, names cannons and cannonballs among those supplies, and the Pinelands Commission says the 1778 Battle of Chestnut Neck, near Port Republic, was fought during a failed British campaign to destroy the Batsto ironworks. [@bcc-history] [@nr-1970] [@dep-wharton] [@pc-history]

From 1784 the Richards family ran Batsto for 92 years, passing it from William Richards to his son Jesse and then to Jesse's son Thomas. Iron making faded in the mid-1800s, though the sources date it differently: the nomination says the furnace cast iron until 1848, while a state teaching guide revised in 2016 says production had stopped by the early 1850s. Glass replaced it. The guide gives 1846 to 1867 for Batsto's glassmaking, and the state parks department's June 2026 page says the works made windowpanes and streetlights for cities such as Camden. The glass business declined too, and Batsto ended up in receivership. [@bcc-history] [@nr-1970] [@dep-teaching] [@dep-wharton]

In 1876 the Philadelphia businessman Joseph Wharton acquired Batsto at a master's sale, adding nearby land to his holdings. Because Batsto no longer made iron or glass, the state parks department's June 2026 page says, it became "a small farming and milling community" under Wharton and one of his family's private estates. The nomination says he gave the mansion its Victorian form between 1878 and 1880, adding the tower, a dining room and third and fourth stories on its eastern section. After Wharton died in 1909, the Girard Trust Company in Philadelphia managed his Pine Barrens lands until 1954. The state then bought them: the nomination and the teaching guide give the year as 1954, and the Pinelands Commission gives 1955. According to the Batsto Citizens Committee, the few residents left were allowed to stay, and the last of them moved out in 1989. [@bcc-history] [@dep-wharton] [@nr-1970] [@dep-teaching] [@pc-history]

## Why it matters

Batsto Village was added to the National Register of Historic Places on September 10, 1971. Its nomination called it "an excellent example of an 18th and 19th Century self-sufficient industrial community." The Pinelands Commission says large-scale settlement of the Pinelands interior began only with the first bog-iron furnaces, in 1765. [@nr-1970] [@pc-history]

People used this land long before the ironworks. The Batsto Citizens Committee says evidence shows several thousand years of land use around Batsto and that archaeologists have found prehistoric remains there. The Pinelands Commission dates the first people in the Pinelands to about 10,000 BC and describes them as predecessors of the Lenape. The state's teaching guide for Batsto, writing about the time after the first Europeans arrived in what is now New Jersey, says that "Little by little the native population was pushed out." Lenape people are part of New Jersey today: the Nanticoke Lenni-Lenape Tribal Nation, in New Jersey, traces its history in its homeland back more than 10,000 years. [@bcc-history] [@pc-history] [@dep-teaching] [@nlltribe]

In 1878, the Pinelands Commission says, Wharton proposed exporting Pinelands water to Philadelphia, and the New Jersey Legislature blocked the plan. The state acquired the Wharton Tract as a state forest that now carries his name. In its page as archived in June 2026, the state parks department puts Wharton State Forest at over 124,000 acres and calls it "the largest single tract of land within the New Jersey State Park System." [@pc-history] [@dep-teaching] [@dep-wharton]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. According to the state parks department's page as archived in June 2026, the grounds are open every day for self-guided tours, and highlights include a working 1852 post office, a working water-powered sawmill from 1882 and a 36-room Victorian mansion, with guided mansion tours offered throughout the year and a museum on the village and the Pine Barrens in the visitor center. The Batsto Citizens Committee says walking tours of the village are typically offered from March through October, depending on staff, volunteers and weather. Don't expect to see the furnace itself: the 2016 teaching guide says nothing remains of it or of the ironworks buildings. Check the official site for hours and current programs.
