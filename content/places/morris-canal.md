The Morris Canal once ran 102 miles across northern New Jersey, from Phillipsburg on the Delaware River to Jersey City on the Hudson. Its company was chartered in 1824. The plan was to bring Pennsylvania coal east, and the route crossed New Jersey's iron-mining country. The first trip from Newark to Phillipsburg was made on November 4, 1831, and the line reached Jersey City in 1836. [@csnj-canal] [@nr-nomination]

Hills stood in the way. Heading west from Jersey City, the canal rose 914 feet to a summit near Lake Hopatcong and then fell 760 feet to Phillipsburg, a total change of 1,674 feet. To manage that, it used 23 lift locks and 23 inclined planes, seven of each in Warren County. The canal's National Register nomination says ordinary locks covered only about 225 feet of the climb, and the planes the remainder, at an average of 63 feet of lift apiece. [@csnj-canal] [@wc-greenway-brochure] [@nr-nomination]

Inclined Plane 9 West sits at Port Warren, in the Stewartsville area of Greenwich Township, on County Route 519 east of Phillipsburg. The Canal Society of New Jersey and the National Register nomination both call it the longest and highest plane on the canal. It raised boats 100 feet. Its length is given differently: the Canal Society lists 1,510 feet to the summit and 1,788 feet end to end, while Warren County's Morris Canal Committee says "about 1600 feet." [@csnj-canal] [@nr-nomination] [@wc-committee-plane9] [@ew-museums-2023]

## Why it matters

The planes ran on the canal's own water. At Plane 9 West, an overhead trough called the headrace flume carried water from the upper level of the canal to a power house, where it dropped 47 feet down a pipe to a reaction turbine in a chamber below. Through a shaft and gears, the turbine turned a drum that moved a looped wrought-iron cable, and the cable pulled wheeled cradle cars along the plane on iron rails. Those rails ran down into the canal at both ends, so boats were floated onto the cradles in the water. Once it had done its work, the water flowed out through a tailrace tunnel into the lower level, to be used again at the next plane. [@plane9-brochure]

At the top of Plane 9 West, the summit worked as a dam that kept the upper canal in. Boats and cradle cars alike came in two hinged halves, so they could bend as they passed over it. Two men worked the plane: a plane tender at the controls in the power house, and a brakeman who rode the cradle car. Plane 9 West had a double set of tracks and two cradle cars, so one boat could go up while another came down. A 2020 county brochure says a crossing took about 15 minutes; the Canal Society gives about 12 minutes for this plane. [@plane9-brochure] [@csnj-canal]

The planes did not start out this way. According to the Canal Society, the first planes, built in the 1820s, were driven by overshot water wheels and were "not powerful enough to be economically successful." Canal engineer William Talcott redesigned them in the 1850s around stronger cast-iron reaction turbines, and the rebuilt planes could carry boats loaded with 70 tons of cargo. The society's timeline dates that rebuilding to 1847 through 1860. [@csnj-canal]

The Canal Society calls the Morris Canal "New Jersey's first industrial transportation system," and gives 1866 as its peak year for tonnage and profits. Railroads and other newer ways of moving freight had overtaken it by the early 1900s; the state took it over in 1922 and drained it in the spring of 1924. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1974 for its significance in industry, engineering and transportation. Its nomination sets the canal beside Pennsylvania anthracite and New Jersey iron ore as things that came before rail crossed the Appalachians, and says the canal "in a very real sense helped make all else possible." [@csnj-canal] [@nr-record] [@nr-nomination]

When that nomination was written, the owner of Plane 9 West had recently dug out the wheel pit to expose the turbine. In 2023, Warren County's tourism site said the Jim and Mary Lee Museum occupied the former plane tender's house. The museum is named for Morris Canal historian James Lee Sr. and his wife, Mary. The Lees made the site their home for many years and dug out the remains of the plane. A 2020 county brochure says the tender's house, turbine chamber and tailrace tunnel are all still in place. As of October 2026, though, the county's listing for the museum says it is closed for repairs, and that visitors will be able to walk the plane and tour the remains once repairs to access the site are complete. [@nr-nomination] [@ew-museums-2023] [@plane9-brochure] [@ew-museum-listing]

Along the old route, the North Jersey Transportation Planning Authority envisions the Morris Canal Greenway as a 111-mile walking and cycling trail from the Delaware River to the Hudson. Parts of it are open, but the authority says it is not yet one continuous trail. [@njtpa-greenway]
