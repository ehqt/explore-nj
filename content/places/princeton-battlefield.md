The open lawns of Princeton Battlefield State Park lie about a mile south of the town of Princeton. On the morning of January 3, 1777, American soldiers led by George Washington fought and defeated British forces here, on land that was then the farms of two Quaker brothers, William and Thomas Clarke. The state's park page, as archived in August 2026, says the battle is "considered to be one of the fiercest fights of its size during the American Revolution." [@abt-battle] [@pbs-battle] [@dep-park]

The battle closed the "Ten Crucial Days" that opened with Washington's night crossing of the Delaware River on December 25, 1776. On January 2, Washington's army held off a British assault along Assunpink Creek in Trenton. That night it left its lines and marched around Lord Cornwallis's army to strike at Princeton. British troops under Charles Mawhood, on the road to reinforce Cornwallis at Trenton, spotted the Americans, reversed course and attacked at sunrise. [@dep-park] [@pbs-battle] [@abt-battle]

General Hugh Mercer's brigade ran into the British at William Clarke's orchard and was broken by a bayonet charge, and Philadelphia militia sent to fill the gap were driven back too. Washington then rode forward himself, and the counterattack he led sent the British line into a rout. Some of the British retreated into Princeton, where troops sheltering in Nassau Hall, at the College of New Jersey (now Princeton University), surrendered. Washington then took his army north to Morristown for the winter. [@abt-battle] [@pbs-battle] [@nhl-nomination]

## Why it matters

Mercer, a doctor born in Scotland who had become close friends with Washington during the Seven Years' War, was a brigadier general in the Continental Army. He was trying to rally his men when British bayonets struck him down. Soldiers carried him to an oak tree and then to Thomas Clarke's house, which had become a field hospital, and he died there on January 12, nine days after the battle. Mercer County, where the battlefield lies, is named for him. [@abt-mercer] [@abt-battle] [@nhl-nomination] [@pbs-landmarks]

In 1772 Thomas Clarke acquired 200 acres from his older brother William, and he later built the house. His sisters Sarah and Hannah lived there with him, as did Susannah, a Black woman enslaved by the Clarke family. After the battle the Clarkes took in wounded soldiers from both armies. The Princeton Battlefield Society calls the house "the only surviving structure from the Battle of Princeton." As archived in August 2026, the state's page describes a seven-room house with much of its original flooring, moldings and windows, furnished in the style of the Revolutionary period. [@dep-park] [@pbs-landmarks]

The Princeton Battlefield Society describes the Mercer Oak as a long-standing symbol of the battle. A 1975 landmark nomination said the tree "reportedly marks the site" where Mercer was mortally wounded, and the society repeats the legend that he was laid against it. The old tree fell in 2000; the state dates its collapse to March of that year. The state's page, as archived in August 2026, describes an oak, grown in 1981 from one of the tree's acorns, standing beside the old stump; the society's timeline says a descendant was planted in the same location in 2001. [@nhl-nomination] [@pbs-landmarks] [@dep-park]

The 1975 nomination quoted Christopher Ward's 1952 history, The War of the Revolution, on how the victory "had an effect on the American cause entirely disproportionate to the number of men engaged." The state calls it Washington's first win on the field against British regulars. The National Park Service lists the Princeton Battlefield as a National Historic Landmark, with the date January 20, 1961. [@nhl-nomination] [@dep-park] [@nps-nhl-list]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. As of the state's page archived in August 2026, visitors can tour the Clarke House museum and see the stump of the Mercer Oak and an oak grown from one of its acorns, a stone patio that marks roughly where British and American soldiers killed in the battle share a grave, and the Colonnade, once the front of a Philadelphia house that the architect Thomas U. Walter designed in 1836, re-erected here as the grave's entrance. A path, partly paved and partly gravel, circles the park, and the Institute Woods next door has hiking trails. The Princeton Battlefield Society offers guided tours. Check the state's park page for current hours and closures. [@dep-park] [@pbs-landmarks] [@pbs-home]
