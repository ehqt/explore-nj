Edwin B. Forsythe National Wildlife Refuge protects more than 48,000 acres of coastal land and water in southern New Jersey, in Atlantic, Burlington and Ocean counties. Its salt marsh stretches about 50 miles along the back bays of the shore, from Barnegat Bay in Brick Township south to Reeds Bay, "just outside Atlantic City." Wetlands make up more than 82 percent of the refuge, and most of that is salt marsh dotted with coves and shallow bays. [@fws-home] [@fws-about]

The refuge started out as two: Brigantine, set up in 1939, and Barnegat, set up in 1967, both meant to protect tidal marsh and shallow bays for migrating water birds. They were joined on September 22, 1984, and named for Edwin B. Forsythe, a former mayor of Moorestown who was elected to Congress in 1970 and served there until his death in March 1984. The Fish and Wildlife Service calls him "a known defender of the National Wildlife Refuge System." The refuge lies on the homelands of the Lenni Lenape. [@fws-about]

Before the refuge existed, a railroad crossed this marsh to carry tourists and goods to Brigantine. A storm on September 12, 1903 destroyed much of its wooden trestle and ended the line. After the refuge was created, the old railroad bed in the marsh was raised considerably to create an impoundment that lets the refuge manage it for migratory birds all year. [@fws-about]

## Why it matters

The Fish and Wildlife Service places the refuge in one of the Atlantic Flyway's "most active flight paths," which makes it "an important link in seasonal bird migration." The agency adds that the refuge becomes more valuable to water birds as more of the Jersey Shore is developed. More than 360 bird species have been recorded here. [@fws-home] [@fws-about]

In winter, large flocks of American black ducks and Atlantic brant shelter and feed in the refuge's wetlands. Brant eat only marine plants. According to the Service, their numbers fell sharply when a mold killed off the eelgrass they depended on, and this refuge was "the last stronghold for the brant," which is part of why it was founded. [@fws-home] [@fws-species]

The piping plover, a small shorebird listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act, nests on the refuge's Holgate and Little Beach Island wilderness areas. The Service describes the refuge as home to one of the largest breeding populations of piping plovers in New Jersey, on what it calls "some of the last remaining undeveloped coastline in the state." [@fws-home] [@fws-species]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The main public area is the refuge headquarters in Galloway Township, which has an Oceanville address, at the end of Great Creek Road east of US 9. It has a Visitor Information Center, and from headquarters, the 8-mile Wildlife Drive, a self-guided auto tour, passes through salt marsh, freshwater areas and woods, and has two observation towers, one at Gull Pond and one at Turtle Cove. The Service says the drive's area is "known internationally as a birding 'hotspot'," and a boardwalk over the marsh looks out toward the Atlantic City skyline. [@fws-home] [@fws-visit]

Farther north, the refuge has trails in Brick Township, on Cedar Bonnet Island in Stafford Township, and at Graveling Point in Little Egg Harbor Township. The Holgate Wilderness Area at the south end of Long Beach Island closes from April through August for the bird nesting season. Check the refuge's official site for current hours, fees and closures. [@fws-visit] [@fws-home]
