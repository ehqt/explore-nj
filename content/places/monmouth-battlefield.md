On Sunday, June 28, 1778, George Washington's Continental Army fought the British army of Sir Henry Clinton across the fields, woodlots and swamps west of the village then called Monmouth Courthouse, now the town of Freehold. A 1975 landmark nomination noted that the heaviest fighting took place on ground inside what was then a new state park; the American Battlefield Trust now puts the park at 1,818 acres. [@fomb-fact-sheet] [@abt-heat] [@nhl-nomination] [@abt-visit]

France's alliance with the United States in early 1778 pushed the British to give up Philadelphia and draw their main army back to New York City. Short of ships, Clinton marched it overland across New Jersey, and Washington's troops left their winter camp at Valley Forge in pursuit. On the morning of the 28th, as the British column pulled out of Monmouth Courthouse, an American advance force under General Charles Lee attacked its rear guard. Clinton turned troops around to counterattack, and Lee's men fell back to the west in confusion toward Washington's main army. Washington halted the retreat and formed a line on high ground called Perrine Ridge. For hours that afternoon the two sides traded cannon fire in what the state's park page, as archived in July 2026, calls "the largest field artillery duel of the war." By evening the British had pulled back, and by morning they were gone. [@dep-park] [@abt-heat] [@fomb-fact-sheet] [@nhl-nomination] [@rwnj-manalapan] [@abt-battle]

## Why it matters

For the army that had spent the winter drilling at Valley Forge under the Prussian volunteer Friedrich von Steuben, this was, in the words of the 1975 nomination, its "combat debut." The nomination said the day showed both sides that Steuben had built an army able to meet British regulars "on even terms." The state's page, as archived in July 2026, says Washington restored a reputation "battered by defeats in 1777" here. The National Park Service lists the battlefield as a National Historic Landmark, with the date January 20, 1961. [@nhl-nomination] [@fomb-fact-sheet] [@dep-park] [@nps-nhl-list]

Sources differ on who won. The American Battlefield Trust lists the result as "Inconclusive." The Friends of Monmouth Battlefield call it "technically a draw," with both sides claiming victory. The state's page calls it "a major moral victory" for Washington and his army. [@abt-battle] [@fomb-fact-sheet] [@dep-park]

According to the Trust, Washington turned down General Anthony Wayne's wish to press the attack late in the day, judging his men "beat out and with heat and fatigue." Accounts of the temperature differ: the 1975 nomination gives 100 degrees, a 2019 Trust article describes 96-degree heat, and the Friends' fact sheet says "over 90 degrees." The Trust's article quotes Clinton's report of 59 British dead "with fatigue." [@abt-battle] [@nhl-nomination] [@abt-heat] [@fomb-fact-sheet]

Casualty figures "vary considerably in different sources," as the Revolutionary War New Jersey site puts it. The Trust estimates 325 American and 381 British casualties, while the state's page says more than 600 men were "dead, dying or wounded" by day's end. [@rwnj-manalapan] [@abt-battle] [@dep-park]

Lee's role is disputed. The Trust's battle summary and the 1975 nomination say an angry Washington removed him from command on the field; a 2021 Trust article by William R. Griffith IV calls that a myth, saying Lee stayed and led a delaying stand that bought the main army time, though later that afternoon Washington handed Lee's troops to von Steuben. Lee asked for a court-martial himself, was found guilty that August, and was sent away from the army for a year. [@abt-battle] [@nhl-nomination] [@abt-lee-article] [@abt-lee-bio]

Monmouth is also where the "Molly Pitcher" story is set. The Friends of Monmouth Battlefield say the only first-hand account of a woman working a cannon in the battle comes from Joseph Plumb Martin, a Connecticut private, and he did not name her. A 2019 Trust article says tradition identifies her as Mary Hays, whose husband, William, served in the artillery. The Trust's biography of Molly Pitcher calls her "more legend than fact," though it says there is "some evidence" that Mary Ludwig Hays fits the best-known story. As archived in July 2026, the state's page invites visitors to "Discover the real 'Molly Pitcher'" at the visitor center and learn how archaeologists found evidence of her. [@fomb-martin] [@abt-heat] [@abt-pitcher] [@dep-park]

The Craig House is one of three farmhouses from the time of the battle that still stand on the battlefield. By the state's account, the Craig family left ahead of the fighting, taking their belongings by wagon along with their children and the people they enslaved. A Friends brochure, drawing on a 1904 history of the Old Tennent Church, says John Craig was serving in the American army that day, and that his wife, Ann, left with their one child and two enslaved people. The same brochure reprints part of an inventory of John Craig's goods, dated August 1825, that lists a "coloured man named Jim" with a value of 75.00. [@dep-park] [@abt-visit] [@fomb-craig]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. As archived in July 2026, the state's page says the heart of the battlefield got back its Revolutionary War fences, lanes and a woodlot in the 1990s. At the visitor center, fiber optics trace the fighting across a three-dimensional model of the ground. Walks of half a mile to several miles use 25 miles of farm roads, paths and field edges. Outdoor exhibits on Perrine Hill, Combs Hill and beside the trails describe the battle, the visitor center has two self-guided hiking guides by the Friends of Monmouth Battlefield, and the Friends lead monthly guided hikes. The same snapshot gives the Craig House "limited weekend hours" but also says it was "closed for the rest of the season." Each June, reenactors restage the battle; the Friends started the event in 1990 and call it "the largest annual living history event in the state." Check the state's park page for current hours and events. [@fomb-home] [@dep-park]
