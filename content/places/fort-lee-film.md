Fort Lee is a Bergen County borough just across the Hudson River from New York City. Its film museum, the Barrymore Film Center, says that "before Hollywood became synonymous with moviemaking," Fort Lee was "the hub of the emerging American film industry," and calls it "the birthplace of American cinema." The borough's website uses similar words: "the birthplace of the motion picture industry in America." A New Jersey Historical Commission classroom guide makes the case for the state as a whole, saying motion pictures were invented and first produced at Thomas Edison's laboratory and studio in West Orange, and calls Fort Lee the film town "once hailed as the center of the U.S. motion picture industry." [@bfc-museum] [@bfc-about] [@fortlee-borough] [@njhc-lesson]

In 1907, Thomas Edison's Rescued from an Eagle's Nest was shot on location on the Palisades at Fort Lee, with D.W. Griffith in his first starring role as an actor. In 1909 the Champion Film Company built what the Barrymore Film Center counts as the first permanent studio in the Fort Lee–Coytesville area, choosing a remote spot in Coytesville to evade detectives working for the Motion Picture Patents Company, a group nicknamed "the Trust." Alice Guy Blaché, whom the center calls one of the first women to direct movies, co-founded the Solax Company in 1910 and finished a Fort Lee studio in 1912. William Fox started his company in 1914 in a studio rented from C.A. "Doc" Willat in Fort Lee, where Theda Bara made her movie debut in A Fool There Was. [@bfc-history]

The Historical Commission's guide says film "infused every aspect of life" in Fort Lee during the 1910s. According to the guide, filmmakers including Griffith, Mary Pickford and Mack Sennett crossed from New York with whole companies of actors to film on the cliffs, stars worked in "rows of great greenhouse studios" in Fort Lee and its neighbors, and the studios' and laboratories' tax payments swelled the town's finances. The guide says California's "more hospitable and cost-effective climate" later drew "virtually all" filmmaking to the West Coast by the 1930s; Fort Lee "languished," and most of its film history disappeared "one studio at a time." [@njhc-lesson]

## Why it matters

The Barrymore Film Center ties Fort Lee to the beginnings of two film companies. Universal was formed in 1912 from a combination of companies that included Champion and Éclair, the American branch of a French film company that had opened a Fort Lee studio in 1911. The rented Willat studio stayed Fox's main base for several years, until 1919, when Fox closed its Fort Lee operations; the company later became 20th Century-Fox. [@bfc-history]

The pioneering African American filmmaker Oscar Micheaux shot Within Our Gates in Fort Lee. The Historical Commission's guide says Micheaux meant it as a response to D.W. Griffith's 1915 film The Birth of a Nation, and the Barrymore Film Center calls it the "flip side" of Griffith's film, told from a Black perspective. Both date the film to 1919, though an essay reprinted in the guide gives 1920. [@bfc-history] [@njhc-lesson]

The borough also credits Fort Lee with a word. It says "cliffhanger," the term for a movie full of suspense and danger, came from the early serials filmed on the Palisades there. [@fortlee-borough]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The Barrymore Film Center, which opened in October 2022, stands near the George Washington Bridge, at the corner of Main Street, Park Avenue and Palisade Avenue. It is named for the Barrymore acting family: in 1900, Broadway actor Maurice Barrymore, who lived in Coytesville, held a benefit for a local firehouse at which his son John, then 18, made his acting debut. [@bfc-history] [@bfc-visit]

Alongside its cinema is a museum whose ongoing exhibit, "Fort Lee: Birthplace of American Cinema," shows rare artifacts, historic costumes, studio photographs and interactive displays, with new items added on a rotating basis. The museum's opening times follow the day's screenings, so check the center's official site before you go. [@bfc-museum] [@bfc-faq]
