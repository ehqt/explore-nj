Walt Whitman spent the last eight years of his life, 1884 to 1892, in a modest two-story wooden house in Camden. The house dates to around 1848 and was built in the Greek Revival style. Both the Walt Whitman Association and the New Jersey Historic Trust's Journey Through Jersey site describe it as the only home the poet ever owned. [@nhl-nomination] [@jtj] [@wwa-history]

Whitman came to Camden in 1873 while recovering from a stroke, and spent years living with his brother George on nearby Stevens Street. When George moved to Burlington, Walt decided to stay put. On April 1, 1884, he bought the Mickle Street house, numbered 328 at the time and renumbered 330 since, for $1,750. The Walt Whitman Association credits "the surprising success" of the 1882 edition of Leaves of Grass with paying for it. [@nhl-nomination] [@wwa-history]

Unable to manage on his own, Whitman invited Mary O. Davis, a neighbor and a sea captain's widow, to bring her furniture and run the household. She paid no rent and was given a modest wage, and she remained with him until he died on March 26, 1892. [@nhl-nomination] [@wwa-history]

## Why it matters

Whitman paid to publish the first Leaves of Grass himself in 1855, and he went on editing and expanding it through his Camden years. In 1891, with help from Horace Traubel, his companion in those years and later his biographer, he published the book's ninth and final version. A 1975 National Park Service landmark nomination called the house "the surviving structure most intimately associated with the Poet of Democracy." [@wwa-history] [@nhl-nomination]

Whitman's international fame drew writers including Oscar Wilde and Bram Stoker to the city to see him, and the Philadelphia artist Thomas Eakins, a friend, photographed him and painted his portrait. In a line quoted by the Walt Whitman Association, the poet said he would "never be sorry I was left over in Camden." [@wwa-history]

Whitman is buried in Camden's Harleigh Cemetery, in a tomb whose construction he began shortly before his death. The house itself passed to the State of New Jersey in 1947, and the National Park Service designated it a National Historic Landmark in 1962. Its collection includes Whitman's letters and belongings, the bed he died in, the death notice that was nailed to the front door, and the earliest known image of Whitman, which the Walt Whitman Association identifies as an 1848 daguerreotype. [@nhl-nomination] [@nps-nhl-list] [@wwa-history] [@jtj]

The house is not open right now. According to the Walt Whitman Association, it closed on December 1, 2025, for major renovations that include a new visitor center, and reopening is targeted for summer 2027. [@wwa-visit]
