Liberty State Park lines the Jersey City shore of the Hudson River, with the Statue of Liberty, Ellis Island and the Manhattan skyline across the water. The state's Department of Environmental Protection calls it "a green oasis in the heart of Metropolitan northern New Jersey." The two-mile Hudson River Waterfront Walkway joins the Nature Center, two picnic and playground areas and the old Central Railroad of New Jersey (CRRNJ) Terminal, which stands at the park's north end. [@dep-park]

According to a 1975 National Register nomination for the terminal, the park's land was "largely a marsh or tidal flat," and high ground began only at Communipaw, which it places about a mile west of the terminal; the terminal's own site was created by railroad building in the 19th century. The department says the park was made by transforming "an abandoned rail yard and illegal dumping ground." Next to the Nature Center lies a 36-acre state Natural Area. Most of it is salt marsh; the department says few tidal marshes like it remain in the Hudson River Estuary. [@nrhp-nomination] [@dep-news-2026] [@dep-park]

The Friends of Liberty State Park trace the idea to 1957, when Jersey City resident Morris Pesin took his family to the Statue of Liberty and was struck by how much closer it stood to his city than to New York, and by the decaying piers and abandoned rail yards behind it. In August 1965 Jersey City deeded 156 acres to the state, which the group calls the park's first parcel. The park opened on Flag Day, June 14, 1976, and the department calls it New Jersey's bicentennial "gift to the nation." [@folsp-creation] [@dep-news-2026]

## Why it matters

The terminal was part of the journey for immigrants leaving Ellis Island. The state's park page says that from 1892 through 1954, many immigrants cleared at Ellis Island came here next to start the rail journey to wherever in the country they were settling. The 1975 nomination notes that the 1889 terminal had "an emigrants' waiting room," and that at the time many emigrants were sent directly from Ellis Island to the harbor's rail terminals to travel inland. It calls Ellis Island the hub of American immigration and Jersey City one of its "spokes" from 1890 to the 1930s. [@dep-park] [@nrhp-nomination]

The building also served commuters. The nomination counts it among seven terminals around the Port of New York where trains met ferries; in the 1889 layout, ferries from here ran to Liberty Street and 23rd Street in Manhattan. The state says that in its heyday the terminal carried tens of thousands of commuters a day to Manhattan by ferry. The station building dates from 1889 and was designed by the Boston architects Peabody and Stearns, but the nomination says the complex "essentially dates from 1914," when a new ferry house and a new train shed were built around the remodeled station. That shed, of a design called the Bush type, was built over 20 tracks, and the nomination calls it "the largest ever built" of its kind. [@nrhp-nomination] [@dep-crrnj]

Trains and ferries stopped here in 1967. The state's terminal page says the railroad declared bankruptcy that year and ended all train service; the 1975 nomination explains the closing as a result of the Aldene Plan, which sent Jersey Central trains to Newark, where riders changed to PATH or Penn Central trains for Manhattan. The terminal is listed on the State and National Registers of Historic Places. As of January 2026, the department said the train shed had been off-limits to visitors beyond its concourse since the park opened, and it announced the shed's restoration, in phases starting with the east side, as one of several projects it expected to open to the public from the second half of 2026 into 2027. [@dep-crrnj] [@nrhp-nomination] [@dep-news-2026]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The park's page, as archived in September 2026, says the terminal is open seven days a week and has a ticket window inside for the ferries to the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island, which run all year. The department says the park is the only place in New Jersey with that ferry service; the National Park Service lists it and The Battery in Manhattan as the two departure points. [@dep-park] [@nps-directions]

Also in the park are the Liberty Science Center on its western side, the Nature Center, which the page says is open year-round, and Empty Sky, the state's official memorial to the 750 people who lived in or had ties to New Jersey and died in the attacks of September 11, 2001. It was dedicated on the tenth anniversary, in 2011. Check the official site for current hours, fees and alerts. [@dep-park]
