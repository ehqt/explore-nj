Thomas Edison's Menlo Park laboratory stood in what is now Edison Township. Today the site is marked by a museum, the Thomas Edison Center at Menlo Park, and a memorial tower topped by an enormous light bulb, in the 36-acre Edison State Park. The state's Division of Parks and Forestry, Edison Township and a nonprofit, the Edison Memorial Tower Corporation, run the center and the park together. [@nps-faq] [@mp-commemorative] [@mp-exhibits] [@mp-about]

Late in 1875, Edison's father, Samuel, scouted the New Jersey countryside for a place to build the laboratory Edison wanted. He settled on Menlo Park, a little-known hamlet on the railroad line between New York and Philadelphia where a real-estate development had failed. The two-story lab was finished on March 25, 1876, with a machine shop on the ground floor and a scientific and chemical laboratory upstairs. Edison promised to turn out "a minor invention every ten days and a big thing every six months or so." The National Park Service calls this research and development laboratory "the first of its kind anywhere," and the museum calls it "the world's first such research and development facility." [@rutgers-move] [@rutgers-lab] [@nps-biography] [@mp-history]

## Why it matters

The Park Service says Edison invented the first phonograph at Menlo Park. In July 1877, after a series of telephone experiments, Edison tried pressing sound vibrations into paraffin paper with a diaphragm fitted with an embossing point. On November 10 he sketched a hand-cranked machine that recorded on tin foil wrapped around a grooved cylinder, and machinist John Kruesi built it during the first six days of December; it worked the first time they tried it. The Park Service describes the tinfoil phonograph as the first machine that could record and reproduce sound, and as Edison's first great invention at Menlo Park. It brought him fame around the world. In April 1878 he demonstrated it at the White House. According to the Edison Papers at Rutgers, an article in the New York Daily Graphic dubbed him "The Wizard of Menlo Park," which became his most famous nickname. [@nps-faq] [@rutgers-phonograph] [@rutgers-wizard] [@nps-biography]

The electric light came next. Edison did not invent the first light bulb, the Park Service says: inventors had been working on electric lighting for at least fifty years before him. In 1879 he made an incandescent lamp that burned long enough to be practical, with a filament of carbonized cotton thread that glowed for thirteen and a half hours. The Edison Papers dates the start of that test to the early hours of October 22, 1879, while the museum gives October 21. Edison and his staff then invented the whole system needed to use the lamp, everything from generators to wiring, fuses and switches. [@nps-faq] [@nps-biography] [@rutgers-lamp] [@mp-history]

In December 1879, which the Park Service gives as the date of the system's first public demonstration, lamps in the laboratory, nearby homes and along the street all ran off a single generator in the machine shop. Over the New Year holiday, sightseers poured off the trains to see it. [@nps-biography] [@rutgers-demo]

Edison's time here was short, though sources end it at different points. The Edison Papers says he "had all but deserted" Menlo Park within six years of opening it and made a formal break in September 1882, when he rented lab space in lower Manhattan. The museum says his laboratory moved out in 1887, to West Orange, and the Park Service says Menlo Park served as his lab for ten years. After he left, the property was abandoned and its buildings decayed, and by 1925 the lab site was an overgrown field with little more than foundations left. [@rutgers-return] [@mp-history] [@nps-faq] [@mp-commemorative]

In 1929 Henry Ford built a reproduction of the laboratory at Greenfield Village in Dearborn, Michigan, using some structural elements from the original complex. According to the museum in Edison, Ford also took the only two buildings that still survived, the glass house and Sarah Jordan's boarding house, to Greenfield Village. [@henry-ford-lab] [@mp-history]

The museum notes that of all of Edison's sites, Menlo Park is the only one where a tradition of commemorating him took hold. A granite tablet from the State of New Jersey was unveiled by Mina Edison, with her husband attending, on May 16, 1925. A steel tower with a big bulb replica on top was first lit on October 21, 1929, for what the museum calls the 50th anniversary of Edison's perfection of the light bulb. Lightning destroyed it in August 1937, while its Art Deco concrete replacement was already going up. The new tower was dedicated on February 11, 1938, Edison's birthday, and was listed on the State and National Registers of Historic Places in 1979. The township took his name too: in a close vote on November 2, 1954, Raritan Township became Edison Township. [@mp-commemorative] [@mp-history]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The museum can be seen only on a guided tour, which also goes into the memorial tower, and the center asks visitors to reserve tickets online in advance. Exhibits include Edison phonographs more than 120 years old, early light bulbs, an 1882 dynamo and a scale model of the laboratory made from the lab's own materials. Outside are the 1925 tablet, the excavated foundation of Edison's office and library, and a nature trail. The museum is on one level and ADA accessible, and the center can be reached on NJ Transit rail. Check the center's official site for current hours, tickets and closures.
