Great Swamp National Wildlife Refuge lies in Morris County, about 26 miles west of Times Square. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, which runs it, calls the refuge a 12-square-mile "natural oasis" in a mostly suburban area. Its own pages disagree on the size, giving 7,853 acres on one page and 7,768 on another. Inside are grasslands, brushlands, mature forest, forested wetlands, marsh, ponds and streams. [@fws-home] [@fws-about]

The Fish and Wildlife Service traces the swamp's basin to the melting of the Wisconsin Glacier. A 2007 article from the New Jersey Geological Survey explains that during the late Wisconsinan glaciation, which it dates to between 22,000 and 18,000 years ago, glacial ice, and then the moraine deposits it left, blocked the gaps the Passaic Valley drained through. Water backed up into Glacial Lake Passaic, which grew to as much as 500 feet deep and stretched north to where Riverdale and Wayne are today. [@fws-firsts] [@njgs-lake-passaic]

As the ice retreated, the ice-dammed lake drained, but smaller lakes held back by glacial deposits lingered. The one the survey calls the Millington stage filled what is now the Great Swamp. It was walled in by the Long Hill ridge on the east and by delta and moraine deposits that had cut off the old valley's route to the northeast, and it was no more than 50 feet deep. Water spilling out through a gap in Long Hill cut down into its cracked basalt, carving Millington Gorge. The deeper the cut, the lower the lake fell, until by about 14,000 years ago it had emptied, and the Passaic River took the gorge as its new course. In 2007 the survey listed the Great Swamp among the wetlands sitting on the old floor of Lake Passaic and its successors, over tens to hundreds of feet of glacial clay and silt. [@njgs-lake-passaic]

## Why it matters

Wilderness Connect says the refuge was set up in 1960 "following grassroots action" to keep the area from becoming an airport. According to the Fish and Wildlife Service, the Port Authority announced in 1959 that it would consider the Great Swamp as a possible site for a commercial jet airport; a Service history article says the press reported a jetport that could cover 10,000 acres. Helen Fenske opened her home to local people planning to stop it, and she became secretary of the Great Swamp Committee that residents formed. John Gottschalk of the Fish and Wildlife Service agreed that if the committee raised money for 3,000 acres, he would match their acreage with money from the Migratory Bird Fund. Through a national campaign, the committee, part of the North American Wildlife Foundation, raised $1 million to buy "nearly 3,000 acres," in the words of the refuge's page; the history article puts the land at 2,600 acres. The foundation started buying land in 1960 to donate to the federal government. The Service says the refuge was established by an act of Congress on November 3, 1960, and formally dedicated in 1964; the history article describes supporters gathering in Basking Ridge on May 29, 1964, with the Secretary of the Interior to celebrate its establishment. [@wilderness-net] [@fws-about] [@fws-firsts]

On September 28, 1968, President Lyndon Johnson signed Public Law 90-532, which made part of the refuge a wilderness under the Wilderness Act of 1964. The law describes the area as "about three thousand seven hundred and fifty acres"; the Fish and Wildlife Service puts the wilderness, the eastern part of the refuge, at 3,660 acres, the figure Wilderness Connect also gives as its total. Both the Service and Wilderness Connect describe it as the first wilderness designated on Department of the Interior lands. [@pl-90-532] [@wilderness-net] [@fws-about] [@fws-home] [@fws-firsts]

In his signing statement, Johnson pointed out that the wilderness sat amid the heavily populated mid-Atlantic seaboard. Setting one aside close to millions of people was, he said, "a departure from the usual concept of wilderness," and he called it a "rare opportunity to provide an island of solitude for those who truly need it." [@lbj-1968]

The land was not untouched. The Service's history article says people had been using the swamp since the 1700s, splitting it into hundreds of wood-lots and draining, farming and logging sections of it. The 1968 law bans roads, motor vehicles, motorized equipment and structures in the wilderness, except as minimally needed for the purposes the area is run for, including emergencies. The Service says it worked to remove roads, buildings and dumps and restored drained wetlands there. [@fws-firsts] [@pl-90-532] [@fws-home]

More than 244 bird species rest and feed on the refuge. In 1966 the National Park Service made it a National Natural Landmark, choosing it as an "exceptional example of the natural history of the United States." [@fws-about]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The Fish and Wildlife Service suggests starting at the Helen C. Fenske Visitor Center on Pleasant Plains Road, which the Friends of Great Swamp place in Harding Township (its mailing address is Basking Ridge). Its exhibits cover the refuge's wildlife, habitats, history and recreation, and the Friends group says a "History Hallway" tells the story of the "citizen-led fight to save Great Swamp from becoming a jetport in 1959." Outside, the Bockoven Trail runs for half a mile around a vernal pool and out to a view of the Passaic River. [@fws-home] [@fws-visit] [@friends-vc]

The Wildlife Observation Center on Long Hill Road has about 1½ miles of boardwalks and trails, with blinds for watching wildlife. In the wilderness area, about 8½ miles of trails are open to hikers on foot only, not even bicycles. The Service recommends waterproof footwear there and warns that mosquitoes, ticks and deer flies may be a nuisance from May to September. Pets are not allowed on the hiking trails. [@fws-home] [@fws-visit]

The refuge holds Canada goose and white-tailed deer hunts in season. Trails stay open in hunting areas, and hikers there are advised to wear blaze orange. Check the refuge's official site for current hours, closures and hunting dates. [@fws-home]
