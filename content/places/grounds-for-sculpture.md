Grounds For Sculpture is a sculpture park, museum and arboretum covering 42 acres in Hamilton Township, the Mercer County town near Trenton. It sits on what used to be the New Jersey State Fairgrounds and opened to the public in 1992. Contemporary sculpture is set out across its gardens, while six indoor galleries host exhibitions that change over time. The park is run as a nonprofit. [@gfs-mission] [@gfs-about] [@hamilton-visit] [@njhof]

Its founder, the sculptor and philanthropist Seward Johnson, was a grandson of Robert Wood Johnson Sr., one of the founders of Johnson & Johnson. In 1974 he set up the Johnson Atelier, a sculpture institute meant to give artists access to production techniques. According to the park, Johnson pictured a public sculpture garden and museum in Hamilton in 1984, with the aim of making contemporary sculpture approachable for people of every background, and he saw the old fairgrounds beside the Atelier as the place for it. Construction began in 1989, paid for, along with the park's sculpture purchases, through public tax-exempt bonds and foundations tied to Johnson. [@sja-about] [@gfs-history] [@gfs-about]

The collection holds work by 150 artists, among them Anthony Caro, Beverly Pepper, Kiki Smith, Magdalena Abakanowicz and the New Jersey sculptor George Segal, and more than 700 artists have had work on view here. Some pieces were commissioned for the park, such as Abakanowicz's Space of Stone and Isaac Witkin's Garden State. Counts of the sculptures differ: the park's About page says "over 300" are outdoors, its history and garden pages say "nearly 300," and Mercer County's tourism site says over 270 artworks. [@gfs-about] [@gfs-history] [@gfs-garden] [@mercer-visit]

## Why it matters

The grounds carry a long fair history. A 2007 article in the Princeton weekly U.S. 1 reported that local businessmen formed the Inter-State Fair Association in 1888 and bought more than 100 acres, including the land the park now uses. Events in its first year included a shooting match between Annie Oakley and Miles Johnson. From the turn of the century, shows by early aviators and by auto racers entertained crowds in the grandstand. The article says the land was sold in 1980, the year the New Jersey State Fair was held there for the last time. [@us1-2007]

Some fair buildings are still in use. The 2007 article dated the Domestic Arts Building, put up to show handicrafts, needlework and canned goods, and the Motor Exhibits Building to the 1920s, and said the museum building came about 20 years later as a shelter for goats, rabbits and other small livestock. Today the Domestic Arts Building hosts exhibitions and the Motor Exhibits Building holds artist studios. The park says the museum building, called the Rabbit Building in the fair era, was moved to its current spot in 1991, and that its Wisteria Pergola stands where one of the curves of the fair's horse racing track used to be. [@us1-2007] [@gfs-exhibitions] [@gfs-about] [@gfs-garden]

In a 2014 WHYY article, Bruce Daniels, then the park's facilities director, recalled that Johnson invited him in 1984 to look at the property. He described it as abandoned for years and overgrown, with leaking roofs and its ornamental terra cotta taken by people with crowbars. The park says it started with 15 works on 15 acres, and that more than 2,000 trees of over 100 species and cultivars have been planted since 1989. [@whyy-2014] [@gfs-garden]

Johnson himself made life-size bronze figures of people doing everyday things. The New Jersey Hall of Fame says the largest representation of his work is here, including his Beyond the Frame series, which recreates classic paintings by artists such as Renoir and Manet in three dimensions. Daniels told WHYY that Johnson's Déjeuner Déjà vu, based on Manet's Déjeuner sur l'herbe, was partly hidden by bushes for a reason: "Seward wanted it to be a surprise." [@njhof] [@whyy-2014]

The art reaches into the town. Hamilton Township describes "Sculptures Along the Way" running from its train station to the park, plus pieces along I-95 and in front of the township library and health building. Johnson's 2014 vision statement for the park says visitors should be "led to the Park by a path of well-placed sculpture." The park counts over three million visitors since 1992 and over 231,000 in 2024; an older township page, citing statistics from 2006 to 2011, puts the figure at 100,000 a year. [@hamilton-arts] [@gfs-founders-vision] [@gfs-garden] [@gfs-culture] [@hamilton-qol]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The park is mostly outdoors and open all year in any weather. Entry is by timed ticket, and the park advises reserving ahead because once it reaches capacity it sells no more tickets on site. Visitors are asked not to climb on the art or trees, not to touch artworks in the galleries, and not to chase, touch or feed the peacocks. Pets must stay home, though service animals are welcome. The park lists Hamilton and Trenton train stations and NJ Transit bus 608 among its transit options. Exhibitions change, so check the official site for what is on, plus hours and tickets.
