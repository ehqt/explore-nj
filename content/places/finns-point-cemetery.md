Finn's Point National Cemetery lies in Pennsville Township, about six miles northwest of Salem, next to Fort Mott State Park and surrounded by national wildlife refuge land. About a mile away, on Pea Patch Island in the Delaware River, is Fort Delaware. The cemetery holds Confederate prisoners of war who died at that fort during the Civil War, along with Union soldiers who also died there, many of them prison guards. [@va-cemetery] [@va-stewardship] [@va-sign-cemetery]

Confederate soldiers captured at the Battle of Kernstown, Virginia, reached Fort Delaware in April 1862. A VA interpretive sign lists crowding, bad food, a lack of clean drinking water, and marshy ground that bred mosquitoes as factors that contributed to the prison's high rate of disease. The VA estimates that 2,502 men died while held there; the 1978 National Register nomination gave the Confederate toll as more than 2,700. [@va-cemetery] [@va-sign-confederate] [@nrhp-nomination]

The island's cemetery filled up fast, and its high water table made it a poor burial ground, so in 1863 the army opened a new one on federal land at Finn's Point, on the New Jersey side. One former prisoner recalled that the dead were sent over by steamboat in pine boxes, and that he and others dug holes "about 6 feet square" and placed "usually about 12" in each. After that, the island was used for burials only when the weather made the crossing unsafe. In May 1875 Virginia's governor, James L. Kemper, wrote to the secretary of war about neglected Confederate graves on Pea Patch Island. Finn's Point was declared a national cemetery on October 3 of that year, and by November the island burials had been moved to it. Workers on the island opened 135 Union and 209 Confederate graves, though 22 of the coffins turned out to be empty. [@va-sign-confederate] [@va-sign-cemetery] [@va-cemetery] [@va-stewardship]

## Why it matters

This is where the federal government buried, and later commemorated, the Confederate prisoners who died at Fort Delaware. The Fort Delaware Society notes that the war-time Confederate dead were buried in mass trenches and pits, that the markers on the Civil War graves were lost, and that, as of 2012, no Civil War burial here, Union or Confederate, had an individually marked grave. [@va-sign-cemetery] [@va-cemetery] [@fds-finns-point]

In 1879 the federal government raised a white marble obelisk for the Union dead, carved with the names of 105 soldiers whose graves could not be identified. The VA's cemetery page describes this Union Monument as a memorial to 135 Union guards, and the Fort Delaware Society says its inscription records the other 30 as unknown. In 1936 the army covered it with a round shelter of six Doric columns under a cast-concrete dome. [@va-stewardship] [@va-cemetery] [@fds-finns-point]

The Confederate Monument grew out of a March 1906 law that set up the Commission for Marking Graves of Confederate Dead, to mark the graves of Confederates who died in Northern prisons. When the commission visited Finn's Point in August 1906, it learned that the records needed to mark individual graves were not available. In 1908 the commission proposed one large monument bearing the names of the dead instead, and the Van Amringe Granite Company of Boston built it. [@va-stewardship] [@va-sign-confederate]

The VA says the U.S. government erected the obelisk in 1910; the 1978 nomination and the Fort Delaware Society give 1912. Its dedication plaque, whose wording the secretary of war approved, reads: "Erected by the United States to mark the burial place of 2436 Confederate soldiers who died at Fort Delaware while prisoners of war and whose graves cannot now be individually identified." Bronze tablets around its base list those 2,436 names. As of 2012, the Fort Delaware Society said its archivists had found about 500 more Confederate prisoners who died at the fort but are not named on the tablets. [@va-cemetery] [@va-stewardship] [@nrhp-nomination] [@fds-finns-point]

Later burials include soldiers who died while serving at nearby Fort Mott and, from World War II, 13 prisoners of war who died at Fort Dix and other camps in the area; the VA says three of them were Russians serving in the German army. The cemetery is on the National Register of Historic Places, entered in 1978 as part of a historic district with Fort Mott. [@va-stewardship] [@nrhp-nomination]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The VA keeps the cemetery open to visitors and still has space for cremated remains. Its directions bring you in through Fort Mott State Park; check the VA's page for current visiting hours. [@va-cemetery]
