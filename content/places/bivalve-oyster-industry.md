Bivalve is a riverfront section of Commercial Township in Cumberland County, where the Maurice River runs out toward Delaware Bay. Rutgers University's shellfish lab calls it "the home port of the Delaware Bay oyster fleet." Here the Bayshore Center at Bivalve runs a museum inside oyster shipping sheds dating from 1904, and those sheds are the home port of its schooner, the A.J. Meerwald. The center describes the 1928 oyster schooner, which is New Jersey's official tall ship, as a "floating classroom." [@hsrl-history] [@bcb-history] [@bcb-home]

Oysters turned into a national business here once trains arrived. A 1994 nomination that put Bivalve's surviving oyster packing houses and docks on the National Register (listed in 1996) dates the railroad's arrival in Port Norris to 1872. Refrigeration later let packers ship oysters already shucked, and the nomination says that by the early 20th century Maurice River oysters were reaching markets from Baltimore to New York and as far west as Kansas City. A second 1994 nomination, for the Meerwald, says Bivalve "quickly supplanted Philadelphia" as the bay's oyster shipping point, and that at the trade's peak the river's two shipping sites loaded 80 rail cars a day for as long as three months a year. [@nrhp-packing] [@nrhp-meerwald]

The work needed big crews. The packing-house nomination says the growing trade soon employed more than a thousand people, split between sailors on the schooners and shuckers and packers on shore, and it notes that the industry drew Black workers to the shucking and packing jobs; the town's Black residents soon founded their own Shiloh Methodist Church. The Meerwald nomination, citing a review of Cumberland County published by the Bridgeton Evening News, says 4,500 people were employed for the bay season in 1929. It also passes on a piece of "local folklore": that Port Norris once had more millionaires per square mile than anyplace else in New Jersey. [@nrhp-packing] [@nrhp-meerwald]

## Why it matters

The Meerwald was built for that trade. The Meerwald family of South Dennis ordered her from Charles H. Stowman & Sons, which launched her at Dorchester on September 7, 1928, designed to dredge oysters under both sail and power. The Bayshore Center says as many as five hundred schooners once sailed up the bay for oysters, and that only "a handful of converted schooners" still harvest them. In 1942 she was commandeered and the Coast Guard fitted her out as a fireboat; in 1947 a new owner renamed her Clyde A. Phillips and dredged oysters under engine power; and from 1959 she was outfitted for surf clamming. Her nomination notes that she lay idle for several years during the Depression, a career it says "documents the economic health of the industry, and that of the country, in the mid-thirties." [@bcb-ship] [@nrhp-meerwald]

The Bayshore Center at Bivalve was founded in 1988 to save and restore her. She was relaunched under her original name in 1995 and entered in the National Register of Historic Places on November 7 of that year, and Governor Whitman designated her New Jersey's official tall ship in 1998. [@bcb-history] [@bcb-ship] [@nrhp-meerwald]

The oyster boom did not last. The Meerwald nomination says an epidemic disease called MSX hit in 1957 and, citing Rutgers scientist Harold Haskin, that an estimated 90 to 95 percent of the lower bay's marketable oysters died between 1957 and 1959. The packing-house nomination reported no successful oyster harvests from 1991 to 1994. Rutgers built a laboratory at Bivalve in 1923; its history credits Haskin with starting a breeding program that produced oysters "highly resistant to MSX disease," and the lab now carries out an annual oyster stock assessment for Delaware Bay. [@nrhp-meerwald] [@nrhp-packing] [@hsrl-history]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The Bayshore Center's Delaware Bay Museum, in the historic shipping sheds, is open to the public on days that vary by season, and its collection of artifacts, recorded oral histories and hands-on exhibits covers the bayshore's history and culture. The Meerwald offers public sails but travels to many ports around New Jersey each year, and the center says its schedules are subject to change, so check the official site before you go.
