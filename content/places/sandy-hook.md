Sandy Hook is a thin finger of sand at the northern end of the Jersey Shore, reaching up toward the entrance to New York Harbor. A National Park Service brochure describes it as a barrier beach about six and a half miles long. It forms the Sandy Hook Unit of Gateway National Recreation Area, a national park that also takes in parts of Brooklyn, Queens and Staten Island. [@nps-shifting-sands] [@nps-gateway-places]

The ocean built the Hook. Along this stretch of coast, waves set up a current that runs north and sweeps sand along the shore, a process called littoral drift. Over thousands of years the drifting sand grew the peninsula and bent its tip toward the northwest into a curving "hook." Channel dredging that began in the 1880s has since changed how the tip grows. When the lighthouse was finished in 1764 it stood 500 feet from the tip; the tip is now about one and a half miles away. [@nps-shifting-sands] [@nps-lighthouse-history]

The deepest water into New York Harbor ran along Sandy Hook, so for centuries ships stayed close to its shore. After shipwrecks on the surrounding sandbars, New York merchants asked the colony for a lighthouse, and it was paid for with money raised by a lottery. It was lit for the first time on June 11, 1764. The National Park Service and the Crossroads of the American Revolution National Heritage Area both call it the oldest operating lighthouse in the United States. It was named a National Historic Landmark on its 200th birthday in 1964, and the Coast Guard still keeps its light on around the clock. [@nps-sandy-hook-places] [@nps-lighthouse-history] [@nps-lighthouse-place] [@crossroads-lighthouse]

## Why it matters

Seawalls and groins built by shore towns to the south slowed the flow of sand toward the Hook, and an undated park brochure, written after 1996, described a "critical erosion zone" on its ocean beach; storms in 1981 and 1982 wrecked a long stretch of the park's main road. Sand pumped back onto the beach in the 1980s was mostly carried off again by the currents within a few years. [@nps-shifting-sands]

Sandy Hook also has a long military past, because the lighthouse made it worth defending. During the Revolution, an American raid failed to knock the tower down, and Loyalists held it for most of the war. In 1895 the Army renamed its fortifications here Fort Hancock, a post meant to guard the harbor against an attack from the sea. More than 7,000 soldiers were stationed here at its peak in World War II, and Nike missiles were based on the Hook from 1954 to 1974. The fort closed on December 31, 1974. [@nps-lighthouse-history] [@nps-fort-hancock]

The federal government had bought the entire peninsula by the 1810s, so Sandy Hook never saw the commercial development found along much of the rest of the New Jersey coast. The entire peninsula, including the part now run by the Coast Guard, is a National Historic Landmark for Fort Hancock and the Sandy Hook Proving Ground, where the Army tested new weapons from 1874 to 1919. [@nps-sandy-hook-places] [@nps-fort-hancock]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. Most of the old fort is now open to the public as part of the national park, with beaches, trails, gun batteries and the historic post to explore. The 1883 lighthouse keepers' quarters next to the tower serve as the visitor center, and rangers lead tours up the lighthouse itself. In summer, a ferry runs to Sandy Hook from Manhattan. Pets are kept off the ocean beaches in spring and summer, when the threatened piping plover nests there. Check the park's official page for current hours, fees and tour times.
