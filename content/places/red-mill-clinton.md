The Red Mill Museum Village covers 10 acres in Clinton, Hunterdon County, on the west bank of the South Branch of the Raritan River. Its centerpiece is the Red Mill, which the mill's 1973 National Register nomination described as a four-story wooden mill beside the river, next to a dam. The grounds also hold a quarry, with its office and stone crusher and sorter, plus a schoolhouse, a log cabin and a working blacksmith shop. The museum describes the site as part of Lenapehoking, the traditional territory of the Lenni-Lenape people. [@rm-home] [@rm-history] [@nr-mill-1973]

The museum says Ralph Hunt put up the first section of the mill around 1810 as a wool mill, a business that failed with what the museum calls "a permanent downturn in the market for domestic cloth." The 1994 nomination for the Clinton Historic District also dates the mill to about 1810 (its building list says about 1808), while the 1973 nomination dated the mill's foundation to about 1763. The village was first called Hunt's Mills. It was renamed Clinton, after New York governor DeWitt Clinton, around the time John Bray and John B. Taylor took over the mill in 1828. The museum credits the Taylors with the new name, and the 1994 nomination credits Bray. [@rm-history] [@nr-district-1994] [@nr-mill-1973]

Later owners ground flour and grain here and made peach baskets on the first level. In 1892 the Clinton Illuminating and Water Company was set up on the second floor to supply electricity for the town's street lamps. After Elmer and Chester Tomson bought the mill in 1905, Chester switched it to grinding graphite, and after the dust caused a public outcry in town he turned to talc. For a short time the building was known as the "Black Mill," and then as the "White Mill." It stopped working in 1928. [@rm-history]

## Why it matters

The 1994 district nomination calls the Red Mill and Dunham's Mill, on the opposite bank, "Clinton's oldest buildings," and says the two mills "caused the town to come into being." The mill is part of the Clinton Historic District, which its 1994 nomination says takes in almost all of the town; the district was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1995. [@nr-district-1994]

Beside the mill is the Mulligan Quarry, worked by an Irish immigrant family. The quarry's nomination says brothers Francis, Patrick and Terrence Mulligan started it in 1844 and bought it from a bank in 1848; the museum dates the purchase to 1848 as well. The Mulligans burned the limestone in kilns to make lime, which local farmers bought to improve their fields. Under Michael Mulligan, the business added ice, coal and railroad ballast, and after a fire swept Main Street in 1891, Mulligan stone helped rebuild the town. The family quarried here into the 1960s. [@nr-quarry] [@rm-history] [@nr-district-1994]

Irish newcomers, the Mulligans among them, settled on Halstead Street, and the upper end of the street came to be called Irishtown; Catholic services in Clinton began in a barn on Francis Mulligan's property. The museum says the lime kilns still stand, along with five early quarry buildings: the office, the screen house, the dynamite shed, the blacksmith shop and the tenant house where workers and their families lived. The quarry's nomination counted three remaining lime kilns and dates them to 1860 in one place and May 1859 in another. [@rm-history] [@nr-district-1994] [@nr-quarry]

In 1960, five men known as the Red Mill Five, including the artist and musician James Marsh, bought the run-down mill for $15,000. It opened to the public in 1963 as the Clinton Historical Museum. In 1964 the quarry came to the museum: the museum says James Marsh bought it and donated it, while the quarry's National Register nomination says it was deeded to the museum through the estate of James S. Mulligan. The name changed to the Hunterdon Historical Museum in 1996 and to the Red Mill Museum Village in 2002. Its collection holds more than 40,000 farm, industrial and household objects. [@rm-history] [@rm-collection] [@nr-quarry]

## Worth a visit?

Check first. As of early October 2026, the museum's website says its museum and grounds are closed for regular visits and open only for the Haunted Red Mill, its largest yearly fundraiser, which runs through October 24, with family-friendly "Happy Haunts" on Saturdays. The same website also lists regular weekly hours at the foot of each page, its home page describes the museum as open year-round, and the closure notice doesn't say when regular visits will resume. Its calendar lists a Winter Village and a Festival of Trees later in the year. The museum's exhibits are on three floors of the mill, and its school tours take in the mill, the quarry's rock crusher and screen house, the blacksmith's forge and the one-room Bunker Hill Schoolhouse. See the official site for current openings. [@rm-home] [@rm-visit] [@rm-events] [@rm-history]
