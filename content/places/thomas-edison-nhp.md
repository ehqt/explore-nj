Thomas Edison's laboratory survives as a cluster of red brick buildings on Main Street in West Orange, where it meets Lakeside Avenue. His family's estate, Glenmont, is nearby. The National Park Service now preserves both as Thomas Edison National Historical Park. [@nps-history] [@nps-faq]

Edison married Mina Miller in February 1886 and bought Glenmont for her, and he lived there until his death. Within months of the wedding, Edison chose to put up a new laboratory in West Orange, under a mile from Glenmont. It opened in November 1887 with five buildings: a three-story main building holding a power plant, machine shops, stock rooms, experiment rooms and a large library, plus four one-story buildings for physics, chemistry, metallurgy, a pattern shop and chemical storage. Over time Edison factories were built around the lab, until the combined site spread across more than twenty acres; during World War I it employed as many as 10,000 people. [@nps-biography]

Edison worked here until he died in 1931. According to the park, West Orange is where he refined the phonograph and the storage battery and where all of his motion picture work took place. He demonstrated motion pictures in 1891, and by 1893 films were being made commercially in the Black Maria, an unusual structure on the laboratory grounds. The Edison Papers project at Rutgers notes that he worked with William Dickson on a motion picture camera from 1888 to 1893, calling it "the clearest instance of a co-invention for which Edison received sole credit." [@nps-faq] [@nps-biography] [@rutgers-biography]

## Why it matters

Both of Edison's major laboratories were in New Jersey, and so was his first. He set up his first small laboratory and factory in Newark in 1871, moved in 1876 to Menlo Park (now part of Edison, New Jersey), where he developed the phonograph and the electric light system, and then built the much larger West Orange laboratory. He received 1,093 U.S. patents. [@nps-biography] [@nps-faq] [@rutgers-biography]

The park also takes on a popular question: did Edison invent the first light bulb? Its answer is no: others had worked on electric light for decades. What set him apart, the park explains, was a bulb that lasted long enough to be practical in 1879 and the whole system around it, from dynamos to wires, fuses and switches. The Edison Papers at Rutgers credit him with inventing the industrial research laboratory, which the park calls "the first of its kind anywhere" in describing Menlo Park, and the Rutgers biography describes the West Orange lab as one that "remained unsurpassed until the twentieth century." [@nps-faq] [@nps-biography] [@rutgers-biography]

The collections here are huge. The park counts over 300,000 history items and about five million archival documents, and describes the whole as the third largest museum collection in the National Park Service. Glenmont, a 29-room Victorian mansion whose decoration the Edisons barely changed, has a decorative arts collection of 40,000 items, from Tiffany & Co. clocks to works by Hudson River School artists. [@nps-history] [@nps-glenmont]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. At the laboratory complex, visitors can go into the visitor center and the main laboratory building, with Edison's library, machine shops and music room, and step inside a full-scale replica of the Black Maria film studio. Tours of the Glenmont house run seasonally, need a tour ticket, and start from the laboratory's visitor center; the house is not fully accessible and the tour involves many stairs. Check the park's official site for current hours, tickets and directions. [@nps-plan]
