Liberty Hall is a mansion museum in Union, and Kean University now sits on what was once its estate. The lawyer William Livingston bought 120 acres here in 1760, in what was then Elizabethtown, and over the following years laid out gardens and orchards and built a 14-room Georgian-style house. A 1972 National Park Service nomination dates the house to 1772–73. The land is part of Lënapehòkink, the homeland of the Lenape, and the museum notes that some Lenape people continue to live in the area. [@lh-history] [@kean-neh] [@nhl-1972] [@lh-land]

Livingston sat in the First and Second Continental Congresses and commanded New Jersey's militia as a brigadier general. On August 31, 1776, under the state's new constitution, the legislature chose him as governor. The museum calls him New Jersey's "first elected governor," and the National Governors Association and the 1972 nomination both describe him as the state's first governor. British forces hunted him through the war, and when he finally returned in 1783 he found the house badly damaged by troops from both armies. He also signed the U.S. Constitution, and he stayed governor until he died at Liberty Hall on July 25, 1790. [@lh-history] [@nga] [@nhl-1972]

His son Henry Brockholst Livingston sold the house in 1798, and several owners followed. Peter Kean acquired the estate in 1811, holding it in trust for his mother, Susan Livingston Kean Niemcewicz, a niece of the governor. She called it Ursino, after her second husband, the Polish nobleman Julian Ursin Niemcewicz. Her grandson Colonel John Kean took it over in 1833 and spent the next six decades enlarging the original fourteen rooms to fifty; two of his sons, John and Hamilton, later became U.S. senators. Mary Alice Barney Kean, widowed in 1949 when Captain John Kean died, set about making the house a museum, and in 1973, after it was declared a National Historic Landmark, she gave it back its original name. The museum began welcoming the public in 2000. [@lh-history] [@lh-about]

## Why it matters

According to the museum, enslaved people were forced to live and work here from at least 1774, when the Livingstons brought several people they enslaved from New York, until 1829, when Susan Niemcewicz manumitted Peter and Sarah Van Horne, the last people she enslaved in the state. The museum says it cannot give an exact number, but it has identified more than twenty-five enslaved people who lived at Liberty Hall, many of them in families. They include Henry, whom William Livingston enslaved and who self-emancipated in 1786, and Bell and Lambert, born enslaved by the Livingston family and manumitted in 1787. [@lh-enslavement] [@lh-enslaved-people]

The museum also traces both families' wealth to slavery elsewhere: Livingston's father grew his fortune by kidnapping and enslaving African people, bringing them into New York from Africa and the Caribbean, and Susan's first husband, John Kean, claimed ownership of about 200 enslaved people as a Charleston, South Carolina, plantation owner. The museum says the site embodies "the contradictions of a nation that proclaimed the values of liberty and freedom but at the same time denied fundamental human rights" to much of its population. The 1972 nomination offers an older view, describing Livingston as favoring gradual emancipation and saying he freed "his own two slaves" in 1787. The museum's more recent research gives a fuller picture: Henry, whom Livingston enslaved, freed himself in 1786 rather than being freed. [@nhl-1972] [@lh-enslavement] [@lh-history] [@lh-enslaved-people]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The museum runs guided tours of the mansion, which displays antique furniture and decorative objects collected by seven generations of Livingstons and Keans, and visitors can walk the arboretum and gardens. One special tour, "Uncovering the Enslaved of Liberty Hall," focuses on the people enslaved here. The walkways are gravel and the mansion is not yet ADA-compliant, though the museum says it is working on that. Check the official site for current hours, tickets and tour dates.
