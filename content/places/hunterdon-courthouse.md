Flemington's columned courthouse went up in 1828 in the Greek Revival style. The National Register nomination for the Flemington Historic District places it at the corner of Main and Court Streets and calls it the town's first building in that style. It describes a "temple plan" building on a high stone foundation, fronted by a pedimented portico on round classical columns, with an eight-sided cupola above. [@nrhp-district]

It was not the first courthouse on the spot. An older county history page, archived in 2017, says a courthouse and jail were built here in the summer of 1791 and burned in February 1828, with arson suspected, and that the historic courthouse was rebuilt on the same site that May. [@county-history-2017]

The county's virtual tour says the building is "Best known" for "The Trial of the Century." Bruno Richard Hauptmann, a Bronx resident, was tried here for the murder of Charles A. Lindbergh Jr., the twenty-month-old son of aviator Charles Lindbergh and writer Anne Morrow Lindbergh. The child was taken from his parents' house at East Amwell in Hunterdon County, near Hopewell, on the night of March 1, 1932, and his body was found on May 12 in a shallow grave in neighboring Mercer County. [@county-virtual-tour] [@case-1935] [@case-1983]

## Why it matters

Hauptmann was arrested in New York on September 19, 1934, after spending one of the ransom bills, and was held in Flemington once New York handed him over. His trial opened on January 2, 1935, with Justice Thomas W. Trenchard presiding, and ran for six weeks. On February 13 the jury found him guilty of first-degree murder without recommending life imprisonment, and he was sentenced to death. [@case-1983] [@case-1935]

In its ruling on his appeal, the New Jersey Court of Errors and Appeals noted "great popular excitement" around the case and "a crowded court room at all stages." According to the court, the jury was kept together under guard and housed at the hotel opposite the courthouse, which also took in reporters and the public. The county's virtual tour says most of the press sat in the courtroom balcony, and that many attorneys, reporters and members of the public stayed at the Union Hotel across the street. [@case-1935] [@county-virtual-tour]

The Court of Errors and Appeals upheld the conviction on October 9, 1935, with no judge voting to reverse, and the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the case. Hauptmann was executed at Trenton State Prison on April 3, 1936. [@case-1935] [@case-1983]

The verdict was challenged decades later. In October 1981, days after an executive order granted the public access to the state police records in the Lindbergh case, Hauptmann's widow, Anna, sued in federal court, arguing that he had been denied a fair trial and asking the court to declare him not guilty. In 1983 the court dismissed her claims on legal grounds, including prosecutorial immunity and time limits, and ruled that none of the legal routes for overturning a conviction were available to her. [@case-1983]

## Worth a visit?

Yes, with some planning. The county says the building now houses its Cultural and Heritage Commission along with space for exhibits and performances, and it lists current and past exhibits and events there. Tours of the historic courthouse and jail are available by appointment through the Sheriff's Office, which says it has led church groups, senior citizens, school students and civic organizations. [@county-courthouse] [@county-tours] [@county-exhibits]

The county's online virtual tour previews what a visitor sees: the second-floor courtroom reached by a spiral staircase, the chair Hauptmann used during the trial, the jury box with its original chairs, a portrait of Justice Trenchard, and Hauptmann's cell in the old jail's block of four small cells. [@county-virtual-tour]
