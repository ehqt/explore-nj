Lucy is a building shaped like an elephant, six stories high, standing by the beach in Margate City on Absecon Island. Visitors enter through spiral stairs inside the hind legs and can climb all the way up to the howdah, the riding carriage on its back. [@lucy-home] [@lucy-faqs] [@margate-about]

The elephant went up in 1881 as a publicity stunt. James V. Lafferty Jr., a Philadelphia-born land speculator, owned sandy lots in what was then called South Atlantic City, and he wanted a spectacle that would draw buyers to them. Philadelphia architect William Free designed the structure, and in 1882 Lafferty received a U.S. patent for an animal-shaped building with rooms in its body and stairs in its legs. [@lucy-ch1] [@slc]

Lafferty first called it the "Elephant Bazaar." The name "Lucy" came from a later owner, Sophia Gertzen, whose family ran it as a tourist attraction until 1970. The Save Lucy Committee describes Lucy as one of three such elephants and says it is "the only one still intact." By the committee's account, Lafferty also built a 122-foot elephant at Coney Island that burned in 1896. [@lucy-faqs] [@lucy-ch5] [@lucy-ch3]

## Why it matters

By the late 1960s the elephant had badly deteriorated. According to the Save Lucy Committee's history, the Gertzen heirs sold the land to developers and, in 1970, donated Lucy to the City of Margate, and a group of residents formed the committee to move and restore it. On July 20, 1970, the structure was rolled down the street to city-owned land a few blocks away, a trip of roughly seven hours watched by thousands of spectators. [@slc] [@lucy-ch5] [@lucy-ch8]

The National Park Service named "Lucy, The Margate Elephant" a National Historic Landmark on May 11, 1976. Its federal record lists art, architecture and invention as areas of significance. The city's own website calls Lucy "our National Historic Landmark." [@nps-nhl-list] [@npgallery] [@margate-about]

Every July the elephant's toenails get a fresh coat of paint, in a color voted on by fans, ahead of a birthday celebration held on the Saturday nearest the anniversary of the 1970 move. [@lucy-faqs]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The grounds around Lucy are open to visitors, and the only way inside is on a guided tour, which includes a climb to the howdah for the view. The interior can only be reached by spiral stairs. Lucy is run by the Save Lucy Committee, a nonprofit, and is a National Historic Landmark rather than part of a national park. Check the official site for current hours, tour times and admission. [@lucy-faqs]
