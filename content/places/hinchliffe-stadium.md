Hinchliffe Stadium is an open-air concrete stadium on a hill above the Passaic River in Paterson, looking out over the Great Falls and the old mill district. The City of Paterson built it in 1931–32, during the Great Depression, paying with a bond issue and later some New Deal money. Its design, by John Shaw of the Paterson firm Fanning & Shaw, mixes Spanish Colonial Revival with Art Deco/Moderne and builds on a layout the Olmsted Brothers drew up for the site in 1931. [@nhl-nomination]

It opened as City Stadium. At its official opening in September 1932 it became Hinchliffe City Stadium, named for Mayor John V. Hinchliffe, who had pushed the project through, and reportedly also in memory of his father, former Mayor John Hinchliffe. Over the years the field hosted integrated high school football and baseball, track meets, boxing, and motorcycle and midget car races, and the Park Service notes crowds there saw Duke Ellington and Lou Costello perform. [@nhl-nomination] [@nps-multimedia]

During the decades when Black players were kept out of the major and minor leagues, the stadium became a home for Black professional baseball. The landmark nomination dates those games from the stadium's first season in 1932 through 1944, and names three teams that used Hinchliffe as a home field at various times: the New York Black Yankees, the New York Cubans and the Newark Eagles. Future Hall of Famers including Josh Gibson, Oscar Charleston and James "Cool Papa" Bell played on the field. [@nhl-nomination] [@nps-foundation]

## Why it matters

The Secretary of the Interior named Hinchliffe a National Historic Landmark on February 25, 2013, for its place in the story of Black professional baseball under segregation; it had been on the National Register of Historic Places since 2004. At the time of the nomination, research had identified 188 venues linked to Black professional baseball, of which only 31 were still standing. The Park Service calls Hinchliffe "one of the few remaining Negro League stadiums in the nation," and a 2022 City of Paterson newsletter called it "one of the nation's last standing monuments to host Negro League Baseball games." [@nhl-nomination] [@nps-management] [@nps-places] [@city-newsletter-2022]

The nomination says Larry Doby, who once lived in Paterson, played on this field in high school football and baseball and in semi-pro and professional games. He became the American League's first Black player in 1947, about three months after Jackie Robinson's first game for the Brooklyn Dodgers; the Park Service and the stadium's museum both describe him that way. He was elected to the National Baseball Hall of Fame on March 3, 1998. [@nhl-nomination] [@nps-people] [@nps-management] [@stadium-museum]

A federal defense law for fiscal year 2015 extended the boundary of Paterson Great Falls National Historical Park to include about 6 acres around the stadium, while barring the Park Service from taking ownership of it. [@nps-foundation]

The stadium closed in 1996, according to the Park Service and the landmark nomination; the stadium's own history page gives 1997. It reopened on May 20, 2023, after a restoration lasting nearly two years. [@nps-nearby] [@nhl-nomination] [@stadium-history] [@nps-places]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. According to the Park Service, the stadium is back in use for concerts and other events, school district games and minor league baseball. The New Jersey Jackals, an independent professional team in the Frontier League, call Hinchliffe home, and the team's site lists 2026 as its 28th season. The Charles J. Muth Museum of Hinchliffe Stadium, focused on baseball history, sits on the grounds, a two-to-three-minute walk from the falls by the Park Service's count. Check the stadium's official site for schedules, tickets and museum access. [@nps-nearby] [@jackals] [@stadium-museum]
