The Wetlands Institute is a nonprofit center for research, conservation and education set in the salt marsh along Stone Harbor Boulevard. Its mailing address says Stone Harbor, but the land is in Middle Township, and the Institute's zoning approvals for its rebuilding plans came from the Middle Township Zoning Board. A salt marsh, as NOAA defines it, is a coastal wetland that the tides flood with salt water and then drain. [@twi-contact] [@twi-history] [@twi-faqs] [@noaa-saltmarsh]

The Institute traces its start to Herbert Mills, who quit business in 1967 to become executive director of the World Wildlife Fund. In 1969, with Mills in charge, the fund bought 6,000 acres of salt marsh in Middle Township. The Institute's facility sits on 34 of those acres, and the fund later handed the remainder to New Jersey's Green Acres Program. The main building, designed by architect Malcolm Wells, was dedicated on September 16, 1972; Mills died on October 21 of that year. For its first few years the Institute was operated by Lehigh University, on a lease of a dollar a year. [@twi-history]

The Institute began its diamondback terrapin conservation program in 1989. During the May-to-August nesting season, staff, interns and volunteers patrol nearby roads and help females looking for nest sites get across in the direction they are already heading. From terrapins killed on the road, researchers can retrieve eggs that are still viable and incubate them; the hatchlings are raised for a year in a "head start" program with local schools and universities, then released. Since 2004 the Institute has also put up barrier fencing along local causeways to keep terrapins off the road. [@twi-terrapin]

## Why it matters

NOAA describes salt marshes as food, refuge or nursery habitat for more than 75 percent of fisheries species, blue crab and shrimp among them. Marshes also slow and absorb rainwater, filter runoff, trap sediment and buffer waves, which protects shorelines from erosion. In a December 1968 speech after a huge coastal storm, Mills argued for saving wetlands as "a natural safeguard and reservoir in times of emergency." [@noaa-saltmarsh] [@twi-history]

The Institute calls the diamondback terrapin the only North American turtle that can spend its entire life in the salt marsh. By its account, terrapins prey on periwinkle snails, which graze smooth cordgrass, the main plant of the Institute's marsh; without terrapins and other predators to keep them in check, the snails can eat the cordgrass down to bare mud. The Institute lists road deaths, drowning in crab traps, habitat loss, sea level rise and predators such as foxes and raccoons among the threats, and says terrapin numbers are falling throughout their range. [@twi-terrapin]

The Institute says its early research led to bycatch reduction devices, which narrow the entrances of crab traps so larger terrapins cannot get in and drown. New Jersey now requires them in waters less than 150 feet wide, a rule the Institute notes leaves out many important terrapin habitats. Since 1997 its researchers have caught, measured and microchipped terrapins at the Institute and in nearby tidal creeks, and they have found several in the local population that are over 30 years old. The Conserve Wildlife Foundation of New Jersey notes that the state halted the terrapin harvest by administrative order in 2014 and 2015, and that a 2016 law made the terrapin a non-game species with no hunting season. [@twi-terrapin] [@cwf-terrapin]

Rising water is now reaching the Institute itself. Its executive director said in August 2026 that the Salt Marsh Trail flooded about twice a year in 2000 but 70 times a year over the past five years; the Institute's Reimagine page says the trail floods more than 65 times a year on average. Its scientists reuse dredged sediment with the aim of keeping the marsh surface from drowning and creating higher nesting ground for terrapins and coastal birds, and on adjacent Scotch Bonnet Island they and their partners have been working to raise and restore tidal marsh. [@twi-summer-2026] [@twi-reimagine] [@twi-health] [@twi-update]

Until it closed to the public in September 2026, the campus featured an aquarium, Terrapin Station, the Salt Marsh Trail, a dock and a 720-foot elevated loop walkway over the marsh that opened in 2014, and in spring and summer it offered naturalist-led programs such as back-bay boat rides, kayaking and guided beach and dune walks. Public programs on Stone Harbor Boulevard ended on September 7, 2026, so the Institute could move out and raise the Salt Marsh Trail two to three feet, work it expected to begin in October 2026 and continue through spring 2027. A proposed new building would then replace the original one, which the Institute says sits low on the marsh and is increasingly vulnerable to flooding. In the meantime the Institute said it would reopen to the public on November 7, 2026, at the New Jersey Coastal Conservation Center, 600 North Delsea Drive in Cape May Court House.
