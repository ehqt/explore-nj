The Delaware Water Gap is a deep notch that the Delaware River has cut through Kittatinny Mountain, where the river forms the border between New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Mount Tammany stands on the New Jersey side and Mount Minsi on the Pennsylvania side. The gap is about a quarter mile wide at the water, nearly a mile wide from summit to summit, and roughly 1,200 feet deep. [@nps-water-gap] [@nps-geodiversity] [@usgs-epstein]

The gap gives its name to Delaware Water Gap National Recreation Area, which the National Park Service describes as over 70,000 acres of river floodplain, forest and ridges in both states; a Park Service geology report gives a slightly smaller figure, about 66,700 acres. The park follows 40 miles of the Delaware, which the National Park Service calls the longest free-flowing river east of the Mississippi, and more than 20 miles of the Appalachian Trail run through it. [@nps-home] [@nps-nature] [@nps-geodiversity]

For centuries the gap has been a passage through the mountain, used by American Indians, by vacationers in the late 1800s, and today by drivers on Interstate 80. The National Park Service says the gap ranked among the eastern United States' most popular summer resorts from the mid-1800s into the early 1900s, when dozens of hotels hosted visitors. [@nps-geodiversity] [@nps-water-gap]

## Why it matters

The gap's rocks began as mud, sand and gravel laid down roughly 450 to 359 million years ago, which were later folded and broken during the mountain-building that raised the Appalachians. Mount Tammany is made mostly of the Shawangunk Formation, a rock unit that includes sandstone. According to the National Park Service, that mountain-building left a zone of weaker rock in the ridge, where headward erosion and stream capture concentrated over time, carving the gap. [@nps-geodiversity] [@nps-paleo]

Ice ages reshaped the area too: during the last 2 million years, repeated glaciers scoured the landscape and left glacial deposits such as till, outwash and moraines behind. In the most recent glaciation, the ice reached about 2 miles past the park's southern edge. A geology field guide by Jack Epstein, listed in the U.S. Geological Survey's publications database, describes the Delaware Water Gap as the one "to which all other gaps in the Appalachian Mountains have been compared." [@nps-geodiversity] [@usgs-epstein]

The park took its present shape because of a dam that was never built. After Hurricanes Connie and Diane caused deadly flooding in 1955, Congress had the Army Corps of Engineers study a dam at Tocks Island that would have created a reservoir about 40 miles long. About 600 families and other landowners in the reservoir area had to give up their property, including families who had lived on and worked that land for centuries. Opposition grew, and in 1975 the Delaware River Basin Commission voted to shelve the project. The land already bought for it went to the National Park Service, leaving 40 miles of the river free-flowing. [@nps-tocks]

The river matters well beyond the park: it supplies water to millions of people on the East Coast, including in New York and Philadelphia. [@nps-nature]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. Kittatinny Point, along Interstate 80, has parking, a picnic area and a boat launch, with a view of the gap from river level. Swimming is not allowed there because the river is dangerous at that spot. For a view from above, the Red Dot Trail climbs Mount Tammany on the New Jersey side. Check the park's official site for current conditions, hours and fees. [@nps-kittatinny-point] [@nps-water-gap]
