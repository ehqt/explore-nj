Just east of downtown Newark, near Penn Station, is the neighborhood called the Ironbound. The Ironbound Community Corporation, a community organization founded in 1969, traces its edges as Penn Station and the Amtrak line on the west, the Passaic River on the north, Routes 1 and 9, the New Jersey Turnpike and Port Newark on the east, and Highway 78 and Newark Airport on the south. By the corporation's count, the neighborhood covers four square miles, takes in most of the city's East Ward council district, and has about 50,000 residents. Its other name, "Down Neck," comes from part of the area sitting on a neck of the Passaic River, according to the history published by the Ironbound Business Improvement District. [@ibid-home] [@icc-overview] [@icc-community] [@ibid-history]

Accounts of the name differ a little. The business district's history, first written by the Newark Preservation and Landmark Committee, says the area is called the Ironbound because railroad tracks surround it. The community corporation credits the "many forges and foundries and railroads that once encircled it." In a 1996 column now hosted by the Newark Public Library, Charles Cummings wrote that no one person can be said to have coined the name, that it "gradually evolved" by the 1830s with the coming of the railroads, and that the "stronger evidence" points to the tracks rather than to forges and steel plants. He added that the section was first known as Dutch Neck and later Down Neck. [@ibid-history] [@icc-community] [@npl-ironbound-1]

Until the 1820s only a few farmers lived here, the business district's history says. That account ties the rise of iron, chemical, brewing, tanning and leather industries to the Morris Canal, whose completion Cummings dates to 1829, and to the first railroads of the 1830s, with German and Irish immigrants helping to supply the labor. Cummings wrote in 1996 that the P. Ballantine brewery, which moved to Ferry Street, had become Newark's largest employer by 1954, with more than 4,000 workers. The community corporation says factories, warehouses and other industrial sites still operate next to homes built for one, two or three families. [@ibid-history] [@npl-ironbound-1] [@npl-ironbound-2] [@icc-community]

## Why it matters

The Ironbound has taken in one wave of newcomers after another. The community corporation lists German, Lithuanian, Italian and Polish immigrants in the 19th century; Black Americans arriving from the Jim Crow South in the Great Migration, along with many Portuguese and Spanish immigrants, in the early 20th century; and immigrants from Central and South America in the later 20th century. It says immigration continues, that two out of three residents came to the United States as immigrants, and that Spanish, Portuguese and English are heard across the neighborhood. The business district names Portuguese, Spanish and Brazilian influences among its multicultural roots. Its history also points to St. Casimir's, a twin-towered church built in 1919 for the Polish area near Pulaski Street. [@icc-community] [@ibid-home] [@ibid-history]

The Portugal Day organizers say Portuguese immigrants began coming to Newark in the early 1900s, with what they call "a significant wave" beginning in the 1950s, and that many made their homes in the Ironbound, which came to be called "Little Portugal." Writing in 1997, Cummings described Portuguese-Americans being recruited at the start of World War I to work in Newark's chemical plants, breweries and tanneries, noted that some came after living in Brazil or New England, and counted six Portuguese-language newspapers published in the city between 1928 and 1933. [@dia-portugal] [@npl-portuguese]

Each June, the Portugal Day festival spans a weekend to honor Portuguese culture and language, with a parade on Ferry Street and performances in Peter Francisco Park. The organizers say it was first organized in 1979 by the Bernardino Coutinho Foundation, that the nonprofit Dia de Portugal Newark has run it since 2015, and that it draws hundreds of thousands of people. The organizers say June 10 commemorates the 1580 death of Luís Vaz de Camões, whom they call Portugal's national poet, and is a day when Portuguese-speaking people are celebrated worldwide. [@dia-portugal]

The community corporation also describes challenges: industry that has caused "significant environmental degradation," leading, it says, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency to classify the Ironbound as an "Environmental Justice Community," and new development that has raised fears of gentrification and of longtime residents being displaced. [@icc-community]

## Worth a visit?

The community corporation calls the Ironbound a "desirable community to live in, invest in, and visit," and the business district says its website is meant for visitors as well as residents. The district says it is home to more than 570 businesses and over 170 restaurants. Ferry Street, which the community corporation calls the neighborhood's "spine," dates to 1765, when the colonial legislature authorized the Old Ferry Road, which the business district's history describes as part of the first direct route between Newark and the Hudson River. Check the business district's site for current events and listings. [@ibid-home] [@icc-community] [@ibid-history]
