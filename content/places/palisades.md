The Palisades are a line of steep cliffs along the west bank of the Hudson River in New Jersey and New York. The New Jersey section of Palisades Interstate Park, in Bergen County, takes in the cliffs, the riverfront below and uplands on top: a strip about 12 miles long and half a mile wide, covering roughly 2,500 acres and laced with more than 30 miles of trails. The park describes itself as "only minutes from midtown Manhattan." [@pipc-home] [@pipc-about] [@nhl-nomination]

The cliffs rise in vertical columns of rock that, the park says, reminded people of palisade stockades. The park also records an Indigenous name for the area, Wee-Awk-En, meaning "Rocks that look like trees," and says it survives today in the name of the town of Weehawken. [@pipc-nature]

According to the park, the rock of the cliffs formed about 200 million years ago, as Pangaea broke apart. Molten rock pushed into beds of sandstone and shale near the surface and hardened into a sill: a layer of hard diabase beneath softer rock. Diabase is an igneous rock similar to basalt; its light feldspar and dark augite give it a "salt-and-pepper" look up close. The U.S. Geological Survey's geologic names lexicon calls this body the Palisade Diabase and dates it to the Early Jurassic. [@pipc-nature] [@usgs-geolex]

## Why it matters

Because the diabase is harder than the rock that once surrounded it, erosion over millions of years stripped the softer layers away and left the sill's eastern edge standing as the cliff face. Ice Age glaciers scraped off loose rock, scratched the summit and dropped boulders carried from distant places, and the Hudson and earlier streams removed what remained of the sandstone and shale. At river level, visitors can see where molten diabase at over 1,000°F turned the rock beneath it into a crumbly, often purplish metamorphic rock. Pieces still crack off, and rockfalls, such as one at State Line Lookout on May 12, 2012, pile broken rock called talus at the base, where soil has collected and forest has taken root. [@pipc-nature]

Quarrying put the cliffs at risk. The park says that by 1850 dozens of small quarries were taking stone for docks and cobblestone roads, and the 1975 National Park Service landmark nomination says quarries multiplied fast after the Civil War, as demand for concrete grew late in the century. In the 1890s large operations such as the Carpenter Brothers' quarry in Fort Lee used dynamite on the cliff face to make crushed stone for railroads, roadbeds and concrete. [@pipc-history] [@pipc-about] [@pipc-nature] [@nhl-nomination]

Opposition first organized in New York, whose residents had the quarried cliffs in full view across the river. The park credits the American Scenic and Historic Preservation Society with the first organized effort in the 1890s, joined in 1895 by the New Jersey State Federation of Women's Clubs. According to the nomination, an offer by the two states in the mid-1890s to hand the cliff face and waterfront to the federal government was rejected by Congress, and New Jersey's quarry owners fought the later bill for an interstate commission hard. In 1900 Governors Foster Voorhees of New Jersey and Theodore Roosevelt of New York signed the legislation that created the Palisades Interstate Park Commission. [@pipc-history] [@nhl-nomination]

The sources differ on J. P. Morgan's gift. The park says he gave more than $125,000 to close the Carpenter Brothers' quarry in Fort Lee; the nomination says he donated the whole $122,500 still needed to buy a quarry property at Fort Lee Bluff, where, it says, 12,000 cubic yards of stone were coming off the cliffs each day. John D. Rockefeller Sr. and members of the women's clubs also gave money. On September 27, 1909, the two governors dedicated the park, as part of celebrations for the 300th anniversary of Henry Hudson's voyage up the river. [@pipc-history] [@nhl-nomination]

The park was named a National Historic Landmark in 1965, and the National Park Service's 1975 landmark documentation calls it an "unusual effort by two states" to protect the Hudson's lower west bank. In 1983 "The Palisades of the Hudson" became a National Natural Landmark; the park quotes the designation as calling them "the best example of a thick diabase sill formation known in the United States." [@pipc-history] [@pipc-nature] [@nhl-nomination]

## Worth a visit?

Yes. The trails range from gentle walks by the river to hard rock scrambles, and there are picnic areas along the shore. At State Line Lookout in Alpine is Point Lookout, which the park gives as the highest point of the cliffs in New Jersey, at 520 feet above sea level; each fall, volunteers there track migrating raptors. Check the park's official site for current hours, parking and trail closures. [@pipc-home] [@pipc-nature] [@pipc-stateline]
